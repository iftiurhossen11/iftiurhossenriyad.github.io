Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - What Is Online Safety Today?
What counts as "online safety" has expanded beyond avoiding strangers or blocking bullies. Today it means understanding how devices, apps, networks and platform designs create different kinds of risk — and matching supervision, conversations and rules to a child’s age and abilities.
What to look at (short guide)
* Devices and access: Phones, tablets, consoles and shared family computers each create different opportunities for contact, content and purchases. A console often limits web browsing but may offer chat in games; a smartphone is portable and personal, increasing exposure.
* Apps and platforms: Social networks, messaging apps, games and forums vary in moderation, privacy defaults and how they connect people. Some emphasize real names and friends; others encourage anonymity and public posting. Each model changes what problems are most likely (e.g., humiliation and viral shaming on public feeds; grooming or privacy harms in private group chats).
* WiFi and networks: Public or poorly secured WiFi can expose devices to eavesdropping or account hijacking. Home networks usually feel safe but still need basic protections (strong router password, firmware updates).
* Content and usergenerated environments: Games, creative platforms and comment threads are full of user content that may be inappropriate, misleading, or harmful — and can spread quickly through sharing and screenshots.
How digital spaces shape risk and interaction
Platforms encourage certain behaviours through their design. A few patterns to notice:
* Public vs. private: Public feeds amplify content quickly and make mistakes visible to many. Private chats feel intimate but can hide harassment or grooming until it’s advanced.
* Ephemeral vs. permanent: Apps that autodelete messages can encourage riskier sharing. Even so, screenshots and shared copies make nothing truly temporary.
* Algorithmic amplification: Recommendation systems prioritize engagement, not wellbeing. That means sensational, extreme or attentiongrabbing content can spread faster than thoughtful material.
* Moderation level: Some spaces have strong moderation and reporting tools; others rely on community moderation or none at all. Lower moderation increases the chance of harmful content persisting.
* Anonymity and identity signals: Anonymity can enable honest expression but also lowers accountability. Realname environments shift social dynamics and may protect some users while excluding others.
Ageadaptable approach: access, autonomy and supervision
Children (roughly ages 5–10)
* Needs: Clear boundaries, simple rules and active supervision. Use parental controls, limit account creation, and keep devices in shared spaces.
* Conversations: Focus on rules about what to share (no personal info), how to ask for help, and what to do if content feels weird or scary.
Tweens (roughly 11–13)
* Needs: Gradual autonomy with coached decision making. Allow limited, monitored experiences on ageappropriate platforms and teach how to recognize scams, strange requests and bullying.
* Conversations: Roleplay saying no, reporting, and talking about peer pressure online. Encourage saving evidence and talking with a trusted adult.
Teens (roughly 14–17)
* Needs: Increased privacy, responsibility and opportunities to practise selfregulation. Discuss public vs private posting, reputation, and how algorithms shape what they see.
* Conversations: Talk about identity, image pressure, consent in sharing content, and steps to take if something goes wrong (blocking, reporting, evidence preservation).
Young adults (18+)
* Needs: Support for navigating realworld consequences (school, work, reputation). Encourage strong account security and media literacy for misinformation and scams.
* Conversations: Treat them as partners in safety — offer guidance on tools and resources rather than strict controls.
Practical classroom and family checkpoints
* Know the spaces kids use: Ask students or family members which apps, games and servers they spend time in and what they like or dislike about them.
* Set clear, ageappropriate rules: Posting rules, privacy basics, and expectations for reporting problems.
* Teach simple technical habits: Use unique passwords, enable multifactor authentication, update apps and lock family WiFi with a strong password.
* Practice responses: Have scripts for blocking, reporting and talking to an adult. Roleplay a short scenario once a term.
Start with curiosity, not control
Open conversations by asking what students or kids enjoy online and what worries them. When adults listen first, children are more likely to share problems early — which makes intervention easier and more effective.
Next steps
Use this foundation to tailor lessons: teach concrete privacy skills (passwords, settings), spotting scams and grooming, and building peer cultures that support bystanders and those targeted. Small, consistent conversations and ageappropriate rules reduce harm and help young people learn to manage digital life safely.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Privacy & Digital Footprints
Why this matters
Every time someone creates an account, posts a photo, comments, or joins a group, they leave pieces of themselves online. Some of those pieces are harmless; others can be used to identify, track, target, or embarrass someone — now or years later. Understanding what information exists, who can see it, and how long it can last helps people make safer choices about what to share.
What counts as personal data (quick guide)
* Identifiers: full name, usernames, email addresses, phone numbers, school, or employee ID.
* Contact & location: phone numbers, home addresses, checkins, geotagged photos, and consistent posting patterns that reveal routines.
* Biographical and profile data: birthdays, family details, workplaces, relationship status, and personal bios.
* Content and creative output: photos, videos, essays, comments, game handles and ingame actions.
* Behavioral and usage data: search history, watch history, app usage, and likes that platforms and advertisers collect.
* Hidden metadata: timestamps, device type, and photo EXIF data (camera model, GPS coordinates) that travel with files unless removed.
Who can see or collect this data
* Direct audiences: friends, followers, or group members who view posts and profiles.
* Platform operators: companies that host content and collect usage data for service features and personalisation.
* Third parties: advertisers, analytics firms, and partner apps that access aggregated or shared data.
* Strangers and researchers: public posts, search engines and archives can make content discoverable beyond the original audience.
Public vs. private — and why “private” isn’t always private
Public posts are visible to anyone and can be indexed, copied, or shared. Private messages and closed groups feel contained, but content there can still be saved or forwarded without consent. Apps that autodelete messages reduce visibility but do not eliminate the risk of screenshots or device backups.
Permanency and persistence
* Screenshots, downloads and shared copies make onceprivate material persistent.
* Deleted posts may persist in cached pages, backups, or on other people’s devices.
* Usernames, tags and screenshots can reassociate past content with a person even after account changes.
How profiles, metadata and context keep things alive
Profiles tie pieces of content together — a username or consistent photo across platforms lets others build a fuller picture. Metadata inside files (like EXIF in images) can expose location or device details unless stripped. Even small clues (school name in a bio, a pet’s name posted publicly) can be used to guess passwords or answer security questions.
Practical steps to reduce risk
* Audit and prune: Review old posts, photos and accounts at least once a year. Remove or archive anything that could harm reputation or reveal sensitive details.
* Lock down defaults: Set accounts to private where possible and limit who can comment, message, or tag. Check settings under sections usually called Privacy or Account.
* Strip metadata: Remove EXIF data before sharing photos (many phones/apps offer this, or use simple metadata removal tools).
* Use different usernames and emails: Avoid reusing the same handle across public platforms you want to keep separate.
* Think before you post: If a post could embarrass someone, reveal private info, or harm future opportunities, don’t share it.
* Control connections: Periodically review friends/followers and remove unknown or inactive accounts.
* Use platform tools: Turn on twofactor authentication, review connected apps, and request data removal where possible.
Quick classroom or family activities
* Footprint audit (15–30 minutes): Students list three apps they use, check one privacy setting in each, and identify one post/photo to archive or delete. Share findings in pairs.
* Photo metadata demo (5–10 minutes): Show a photo’s EXIF data (no real student photos) and discuss what extra details it reveals. Then demonstrate removing metadata before sharing.
* Role play: One student plays a recruiter searching a candidate online; another plays the candidate. Discuss what online traces make a good or poor impression.
Conversation prompts for caregivers and teachers
* "Which three apps do you use most and why? Who can see what you post there?"
* "If a college or employer searched your name, what would they find? Is that what you want them to see?"
* "Would you be comfortable if a stranger saved that photo or message and showed it to others?"
One practical starter checklist (use at home or in class)
* Check and set accounts to private where appropriate.
* Review friend/follower lists and remove unknown contacts.
* Update bios to remove sensitive details (home address, exact school year).
* Turn off automatic location tagging and remove EXIF data from photos before posting.
* Search your name and discuss surprising results.
Start with small, teachable steps
Begin with a short cleanup and a single settings check rather than a long lecture. Small wins (removing location tags, changing a bio) build confidence and make broader conversations about privacy easier.
Where to go next
After learning what a digital footprint is and how data persists, practise concrete skills: walk through privacy settings on a chosen platform, run a class footprint audit, and create a personal checklist students can revisit each term.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Passwords, MFA & Basic Account Security
Why this matters
Passwords are the first line of defense for online accounts. Weak or reused passwords let attackers move from one account to another. Multifactor authentication (MFA) adds a second barrier so a stolen password alone is not enough. Together with good recovery plans and password managers, these simple habits drastically reduce the chance of account takeover.
Strong passwords vs passphrases
A strong password is about length and unpredictability more than punctuation. Two approaches work well:
* Short, complex passwords are hard to remember and easy to reuse — avoid relying on these.
* Passphrases: combine 3–5 unrelated words into a phrase (for example: blue-bicycle-mango-cave). Passphrases are long, memorable, and much harder for attackers to guess.
Practical tips for creating passphrases
* Use at least three random words; four is better for important accounts.
* Add a short memorable number or symbol if a site requires it (blue-bicycle-7).
* Avoid common phrases, predictable substitutions (p@ssw0rd) or using personal details (names, birthdays).
* Never reuse the same passphrase across important accounts (email, banking, school accounts).
Why use a password manager
Password managers generate and store unique strong passwords for each account, so you only need to remember one master password (ideally a long passphrase). They also fill logins automatically and can store secure notes (license keys, recovery codes).
Tips when picking or using a password manager
* Choose a trusted solution (browser builtin vaults or dedicated apps) and enable its sync only through secure means.
* Use a long master passphrase and never share it.
* Enable MFA on the password manager itself.
* Teach students to treat saved passwords like keys: don’t hand them to friends or post screenshots.
Multifactor authentication (MFA): what it is and how to choose
MFA requires something you know (password) plus something you have (a device) or something you are (biometrics). Common methods:
* SMS codes: convenient but vulnerable to SIMswapping and interception.
* Authenticator apps: generate timebased codes (recommended for most accounts).
* Push notifications: the account sends a prompt to your device to approve a login.
* Hardware security keys: physical devices that offer the strongest protection.
Recommendation: prefer authenticator apps or hardware keys over SMS when available. Always keep backup methods (backup codes or a secondary authenticator) in a secure place.
Quick setup checklist (general)
* Go to the account’s Settings or Security page.
* Find Twofactor authentication / 2FA / Multifactor authentication and choose a method.
* If using an authenticator app, scan the QR code and save the backup/recovery codes in a secure place.
* Test logging in from another device to confirm everything works.
Account recovery and recovery options
* Save backup codes in a password manager or printed and stored securely.
* Avoid relying only on password resets to a single email or phone you can lose access to. Add a secondary email or recovery phone when possible.
* Review and update recovery info after changing phones or contact details.
Simple classroom or family activities
* Passphrase practice (10–15 minutes): Have students create a passphrase from four unrelated words and explain why it’s strong. Discuss what would make a passphrase weak.
* MFA walkthrough (15–20 minutes): Pick one common platform, show where to turn on MFA, and have each student enable it on a personal or demo account. Emphasize saving backup codes.
* Password manager demo (20 minutes): Demonstrate generating a password, saving it, and autofilling a login. If policies allow, encourage families to try a password manager at home.
Short scripts for teachers and caregivers
* To introduce passphrases: “Longer is safer. Try four random words you can picture — it’s easier to remember than a jumbled password.”
* To encourage MFA: “Turning on 2FA is like adding a second lock to the door. Even if someone guesses the key, they still need the second lock.”
* If a student loses access: “Stop, don’t create a new account yet. Try the recovery steps, use saved backup codes, and then update passwords and check connected apps.”
Final practical checklist (give to students/families)
* Use a unique passphrase for important accounts.
* Turn on MFA for email, social, school and banking accounts. Prefer authenticator apps or hardware keys.
* Use a password manager to store and generate passwords.
* Save backup/recovery codes securely and update recovery contacts after major changes (new phone, new email).
* Review account security settings once every six months.
Start with the most important accounts
If you or your students set up only one thing today, enable MFA on the primary email account. Email is the key to many other services, so protecting it provides outsized benefits.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Spotting Common Online Risks
Why this lesson matters
Everyone who uses the internet encounters risks. Many harms — financial scams, account takeovers, grooming, and abusive messages — start with small, believable actions: a convincing message, an overshare, or a link that looks safe. Learning to recognise common signs and practising a simple triage routine (ignore, block, report, or escalate) helps young people respond quickly and stay safer online.
Common scams, phishing and social engineering — what to look for
* Unusual urgency: Messages that pressure you to act now ("Your account will be closed in 10 minutes") are classic red flags.
* Unexpected requests for money or gifts: Any request for gift cards, transfers, or financial help from someone you know but through a new channel should be verified first.
* Strange or mismatched sender details: Check the sender’s email or profile carefully — small typos, extra characters, or different domains (e.g., paypa1.com vs paypal.com) are suspicious.
* Links that don’t match the message: Hovering over a link (on desktop) or longpressing (on mobile) can reveal a different destination than the text suggests.
* Requests for passwords or security codes: Legitimate services never ask for your password or the onetime codes they just sent you.
* Emotional manipulation: Scammers will try to make you feel guilty, scared, or excited to get you to act without thinking.
* Fake verification and impersonation: Profile pictures, names or badges copied from real people to seem authentic. Always check with the real person using a different method.
Risks from strangers and oversharing
* Stranger contact: Direct messages from accounts you don’t know can be harmless, but any invitation to meet, share private photos, or move a conversation to a lessmoderated app should be treated cautiously.
* Oversharing: Posting home addresses, school names, routines, or photos with geotags gives attackers material they can use to guess passwords, target scams, or plan harassment.
* Friend requests from fake accounts: New accounts with few posts, generic bios, or a single mutual friend may be attempts to enter your network and gather personal info.
Suspicious links, attachments and files
* Files with strange extensions: Avoid opening .exe, .scr, or other executable files sent by people you don’t trust. Images and documents can also carry malicious macros — when in doubt, don’t open.
* Shortened URLs and redirectors: Services that shorten links (bit.ly, tinyurl) hide the final destination. Use preview features or a linkexpander tool before clicking.
* Unexpected attachments: If someone you know sends an attachment you weren’t expecting, confirm with them via another channel before opening.
Quick triage: What to ignore, block, or report
* Pause and evaluate: Don’t click, download, or reply immediately. Take a breath and read the whole message.
* Ignore when:
* The message is clearly spam or a random ad with no personal info asked.
* The content is mildly annoying but not threatening (repeat promo messages, chain posts).
* Block when:
* The sender is harassing, abusive, or repeatedly contacts you after you asked them to stop.
* The account is clearly fake and trying to join your friend list or server.
* Report when:
* The message involves threats, sexual content involving minors, explicit scams, or impersonation designed to defraud.
* A stranger asks to meet in person or requests private photos.
* Escalate and preserve evidence when:
* You or someone’s safety is at risk, or if abuse is ongoing. Take screenshots, note timestamps and URLs, and share them with a trusted adult, school staff, or platform support.
Short, ageadaptable scripts to use right away
* For young children: “I don’t open messages from people I don’t know. I’ll show an adult.”
* For tweens: “That link looks weird — I’m not clicking. I’ll ask a parent/teacher to check it.”
* For teens: “I’m saving a screenshot and reporting this. Don’t engage — blocking is better.”
* For caregivers/teachers: “Stop, don’t delete anything. Take a screenshot, block the sender if needed, and we’ll report it together.”
Classroom or family activities (quick to run)
* Scavenger checklist (10 minutes): Students list three suspicious things they might see in a message (urgency, link mismatch, request for codes) and explain how they would respond.
* Role play (15 minutes): One student acts as a scammer asking for help or money; another practices the script to refuse and get an adult.
* Link investigation demo (5–10 minutes): Show how to preview a link on desktop/mobile and how to check a sender’s profile for red flags.
Practical prevention steps to teach and practise
* Never share passwords, verification codes, or personal security information.
* Turn off automatic location in photos and social apps; remove EXIF data before posting photos publicly.
* Use privacy settings to limit who can message or follow young accounts.
* Teach students how to save evidence (screenshots, message links) and where to go for help (trusted adult, school counselor, platform safety center).
When to involve authorities
If a message contains threats of violence, persistent sexual advances toward minors, or clear attempts to steal money or identity, involve school administrators and local law enforcement. If unsure, ask a designated school contact or a caregiver for guidance before dismissing it.
When in doubt, pause and ask
A quick pause plus a check with a trusted adult prevents most mistakes. Encourage the habit: don’t click, don’t reply, and don’t delete — ask first.
Next steps
Practice these signs with short scenarios and run a regular class/family checklist: review privacy settings, check friend lists for unknown accounts, and practise the triage routine once a term so responses become automatic.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Being Kind Online — Digital Empathy
Why kindness online matters
Digital interactions shape relationships, reputations and classroom culture. A single offhand comment can be shared widely and cause lasting harm, while small acts of support — a thoughtful reply, a helpful tag, a private checkin — can make someone feel seen and safe. Teaching digital empathy gives students tools to communicate clearly, manage conflict respectfully, and use platform mechanics (likes, replies, shares) with care.
Reading tone, intent and context in chats
Written messages lack many nonverbal cues we rely on facetoface. That means tone can be misread and intent can be unclear. Help students notice three layers:
* Tone (how it sounds): Is the message playful, sarcastic, angry, or neutral? Punctuation, capitalization and emoji change perceived tone.
* Intent (what the sender meant): People sometimes make jokes or experiment with style online; intent matters but is not always visible.
* Context (where and when it was posted): A joke between close friends may not land in a public comment or with someone who lacks shared background.
Simple habits to reduce misunderstandings
* Pause and reread before sending. If a message could be read two ways, clarify it first.
* Use short clarifiers: start with Quick note: or Just joking — when tone might be ambiguous.
* Prefer plain language for difficult topics. Save sarcasm and inside jokes for spaces where everyone shares the context.
* Add a checkin: Did that make sense? or Was that okay to say? invites correction before things escalate.
Respectful disagreement and conflict skills
Disagreements are normal and can be constructive when handled respectfully. Teach a repeatable pattern students can use:
* Name the idea, not the person: I disagree with the point about X, not with you.
* Explain your view concisely and give an example or source if helpful.
* Ask a question to invite dialogue: Can you say more about why you think that?
* Agree to disagree if needed and set boundaries: We can leave it here if this isn’t resolving.
Short scripts for different ages
* Young child: “I don’t like that. Please stop.”
* Tween: “I see it differently. Here’s why — can we talk about this facetoface?”
* Teen: I disagree with your point about X. I looked into Y which says… Let’s keep this respectful.
* Teacher/caregiver: Thanks for sharing. I hear your concern; here’s how I see it. Let’s talk more after class.
Inclusive language and avoiding assumptions
Inclusive language makes people feel respected and reduces accidental exclusion. Key practices:
* Use people’s chosen names and pronouns. If unsure, ask privately: What name and pronouns do you use online?
* Avoid assumptions about background, ability, living situation, religion, or family structure in examples and jokes.
* Replace labels that reduce someone to a single trait with neutral descriptions (for example, say student with dyslexia rather than dyslexic kid).
* Avoid casual slurs or normalized insults — what’s funny to one group can harm another.
How reactions — likes, comments and shares — help or harm
Social feedback is powerful. A like or share can amplify support or spread mistakes.
* Likes: A quick way to affirm someone, but they can be ambiguous. Liking a problematic post may be read as endorsement.
* Comments: Public replies shape the tone of a discussion. Thoughtful comments can redirect a conversation; reactive comments can fuel escalation.
* Shares: Sharing expands an audience. Before resharing, ask: Does this respect the original poster’s privacy? Could it harm someone if it spreads?
Practical rules for using platform reactions
* If you’re unsure whether to like or share, don’t — consider a private message instead.
* Use replies to add value (resources, support, clarification) rather than to criticize publicly.
* Before forwarding or resharing content that involves a person, ask for permission or remove identifying details.
Responding when harm happens — a short restorative script
When someone realizes they’ve hurt another person online, teach a compact response that focuses on repair:
* Acknowledge: I’m sorry my comment hurt you.
* Take responsibility: I didn’t mean to be cruel — I was careless.
* Repair: I’ll delete the post and message you privately.
* Commit: I’ll think before I post in the future.
Classroom activities and quick practices
* Tone translation (10–15 minutes): Give students short messages with ambiguous tone. In groups, rewrite each message in three clearer ways (supportive, neutral, confrontational) and discuss effects.
* Comment audit (10 minutes): Look at anonymous sample posts and brainstorm three kinds of comments that would help and three that would harm. Discuss why.
* Empathy map (15 minutes): For a recent classroom disagreement, draw an empathy map: what did each person see, think, feel, and want? Use it to plan a respectful conversation.
Conversation starters for teachers and families
* “What makes a message feel friendly or mean to you?”
* “If a post makes you uncomfortable, what would you want someone to do for you?”
* “When is it better to reply publicly vs. message someone privately?”
Assessment and reflection prompts
* Ask students to keep a oneweek journal of times they used a reaction (like/comment/share) and whether it helped or hurt. Discuss patterns in class.
* Roleplay a difficult conversation and reflect: What wording kept the focus on ideas rather than identity?
Model the behaviour you want to see
Adults and student leaders should practise clear, kind replies and visible repair when they make mistakes. Modelling empathetic responses teaches more effectively than rules alone.
Wrap up
Digital empathy is a practice, not a onetime lesson. Short, regular activities — tone checks, comment audits and restorative scripts — help students develop habits that make online spaces safer and more supportive for everyone.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - AgeAdaptable Rules & Conversation Starters
Why this matters
Clear, ageappropriate rules and short conversation starters help families and classrooms move from onetime lectures to ongoing habits. Rules create predictable boundaries; conversation prompts make it easy to open dialogue, troubleshoot problems and build trust.
Ageadaptable rules (simple, actionable)
Children (ages ~5–10)
* Device location: Keep shared devices in common areas at set times (e.g., after homework and before bedtime).
* Supervised access: Accounts and apps require adult setup/approval; no private chats without a parent/teacher present.
* Basic sharing rule: Don’t share names, addresses, school names, or photos of others without permission.
* Time limits: Set clear screen time limits (use timers) and encourage one techfree routine each day (family meal, bedtime winddown).
Tweens (ages ~11–13)
* Guided independence: Allow some solo time online with agreed apps; keep accounts visible to a parent or educator.
* Privacy basics: Use private accounts when possible; check friend lists weekly and remove unknown contacts.
* Content rule: Pause before posting — if it could embarrass someone or affect school, don’t post.
* Reporting: Save evidence and tell a trusted adult if something worrying happens; do not engage with harassers.
Teens (ages ~14–17)
* Negotiated autonomy: Teen has more privacy but agrees to occasional checkins and to share any safety concerns.
* Reputation rule: Treat posts as part of a public record — ask, “Would I want an employer or teacher to see this?”
* Security expectation: Use unique passwords, enable MFA for school and email accounts, and update recovery options after major changes.
* Respectful disagreement: Public disagreements should stay about ideas, not people — use private messages for sensitive conversations.
Young adults (18+)
* Partnership model: Offer guidance, resources and options rather than hard rules; focus on problemsolving and media literacy.
* Consequence awareness: Discuss realworld consequences (academic, employment, legal) of online behaviour.
* Support plan: Encourage regular security checks, privacy audits and reflective practices for wellbeing (screen breaks, sleep hygiene).
Short scripts and conversation starters
Opening neutral conversations
* Kids/children: "What was the best thing you saw online today? Was anything weird or scary?"
* Tweens: "Show me one app you like — what do you like about it? Who do you usually talk to there?"
* Teens: "How do you feel after using [app]? Does it make you anxious, excited, or something else?"
* Young adults: "Have you changed any privacy or security settings recently? Want help reviewing them?"
Addressing a risky post or message
* Child: "I’m worried about this. Let’s not reply — I’ll help."
* Tween: "That message looks weird. Don’t click the link; can I see it so we can check together?"
* Teen: "If you want, I can help you screenshot and report this. You don’t have to handle it alone."
Negotiating limits (collaborative tone)
* "Let’s try a twoweek experiment: device curfew at 9 PM and no phones at the table. We’ll check how everyone feels after two weeks."
* "If you show me you can follow these rules for a month, we’ll revisit the bedtime and app list together."

Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Discord — Servers, Roles, and Safety
Why this matters
Discord mixes public and private spaces: servers (group hubs) can feel like a forum, while direct messages and private voice/video chats feel intimate. That mix creates opportunities for friendships, gaming coordination and creative collaboration — and for scams, unwanted contact, and grooming. Understanding how servers, roles and privacy settings work helps educators, caregivers and young people keep interactions safer.
How Discord is organised (quick guide)
* Servers: A server is a place people join by invite. Each server contains channels and settings that shape who can read, write, and join voice/video sessions.
* Channels: Text channels (conversation by topic) and voice channels (realtime audio, sometimes with video or screen share). Channels can be public to the whole server or restricted to certain roles.
* Roles and permissions: Roles control what members can do (post, moderate, create invites). Server owners assign roles to manage access and responsibility; permissions cascade from roles to individual channels.
* Invites and invite links: Join links can be set to never expire, expire after a time, or limit the number of uses. Anyone with a link can potentially join unless the server restricts it.
* Direct messages (DMs): Private 1:1 or small group chats. DMs feel private but can be used to move conversations out of moderated spaces.
Key moderation and safety tools
* Community Server tools: Enabling Community unlocks verification gates, membership screening (rules agreement), and moderation features.
* Verification level: Servers can require emailverified accounts or phone verification before allowing messages — higher levels reduce anonymous or spam accounts.
* Explicit Content Filter & Media Settings: Servers and users can enable filters that scan images for explicit content and block or warn about agerestricted media.
* Audit Log: Records who changed roles, deleted messages, or created invites. Useful when investigating incidents.
* AutoMod and moderation bots: Automate detection of profanity, spam, or links; some bots can quarantine content, mute users, or autodelete messages.
* Role limits & invite control: Lock invite creation to trusted roles and expire invite links quickly. Require 2FA for moderators/admins when possible.
Practical privacy and account settings (user side)
* Friend requests: Set Who can add you as a friend to Friends of Friends or No one to reduce unsolicited friend requests.
* Server privacy: Turn off Allow direct messages from server members in a user’s privacy settings to prevent DMs from people met only in servers.
* Notifications: Mute servers or channels to reduce attentionseeking behaviour and anxiety; customise @mentions so only important pings get through.
* Account security: Use a strong password, enable twofactor authentication (2FA) on your account, and avoid sharing login details.
Risks to watch for (practical signs)
* Rapid escalation to DMs: Someone pushing to move from a moderated channel into private DMs, calls or lessermoderated apps is a common grooming pattern.
* Requests for photos, gifts, or financial help: Any request for money, gift cards, or private images should be treated as a red flag.
* Closed invite loops: Using shortlived or private invite links to create isolated groups where moderation can't see what’s happening.
* Role exploitation: Bad actors may seek moderator roles to silence others, hide logs or create misleading permissions.
* Unmoderated voice/video: Voice channels can host abusive or sexual behaviour that leaves little trace; screenshots aren’t possible for audio.
Short scripts: what to say and do
* For a child: “I don’t talk to people online without checking with an adult first. I’ll show you the message.”
* For a tween: “That person wants to DM me — I’m not going alone. Can you check it with me?”
* For a teen: “I’m saving screenshots and blocking this user. Can we report and review the server settings together?”
* For caregivers/teachers: “Stop, don’t delete anything. Take screenshots, note timestamps and user IDs, then we’ll check the Audit Log and report to platform support if needed.”
Classroom and family activities
* Server walkthrough (15–20 minutes): Create a private demo server. Walk students through Channels, Roles, Invites and Audit Log so they recognise these parts in real servers.
* Invite audit (10 minutes): Have students list three servers they’re in and check who can create invites and whether the links expire. Discuss one change to reduce risk.
* Roleplay escalation (15 minutes): In small groups, practise a scenario where a member asks to DM a minor and another student uses the scripts above to refuse, save evidence and tell an adult.
Incident checklist (quick to follow)
* Pause — don’t delete the chat.
* Save evidence — screenshots, message links, usernames and timestamps.
* Block the user and remove suspicious invites.
* Check server Audit Log and invite settings; restrict who can create invites.
* Report to Discord using Report options and to school/caregiver if the target is a minor.
* If safety is at risk (sexual exploitation, threats, ongoing harassment), contact local authorities and school administrators.
When moderation tools aren’t enough
If a server is unmoderated, abusive, or used to groom minors, remove children from that space and report it. If you suspect illegal activity (sexual exploitation of minors, threats of violence, explicit grooming), escalate to law enforcement and keep records of all evidence.
Protect privacy when preserving evidence
When saving messages for a report, avoid reposting private content publicly. Store screenshots in a secure place and share them only with the trusted adult, school staff or platform support handling the incident.
Wrap up — practical starter checklist
* Lock invite creation to trusted roles and set short expiry times.
* Enable server verification or membership screening for public servers.
* Teach users to turn off DMs from server members they don’t know.
* Require 2FA for moderators and use audit logs to monitor changes.
* Practice the "Pause, Save, Tell" routine so responses become automatic.
These steps help keep Discord a place for community and play while reducing the common risks that come with private chats and unmoderated spaces.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Reddit — Subreddits, Anonymity, and Community Culture
Why this matters
Reddit is a large collection of topicfocused communities (subreddits). It combines public discussion, anonymity, and strong community moderation — a mix that supports honest conversation and niche support networks but also enables mob behaviour, harassment, and the rapid spread of misleading or harmful content. Educators and caregivers who understand how subreddits, moderation, and anonymity work can better help young people use Reddit safely and thoughtfully.
How Reddit is organised
* Subreddits: Each subreddit (r/example) is a community centered on a topic, hobby, school, or interest. Subreddits have their own rules and culture; what’s acceptable in one may be banned in another.
* Posts and comments: Users submit posts (links, text, images) that others comment on. Upvotes and downvotes influence visibility through ranking.
* Moderation teams: Volunteer moderators set and enforce subreddit rules. They can remove posts, ban users from the subreddit, and configure automated moderation.
* Sitewide vs community rules: Reddit has platformwide policies (harassment, sexual content, API misuse) but many moderation choices are left to individual subreddit communities.
* Anonymity and accounts: Reddit allows pseudonymous accounts with no realname requirement. Users can create throwaway accounts for private matters or longterm usernames tied to a public posting history.
Anonymity: benefits and risks
Anonymity on Reddit has real upsides:
* Safe sharing: People discuss personal problems, mental health, or sensitive experiences in supportive communities without revealing identity.
* Honest opinions: Users can ask questions or share mistakes without immediate social consequences.
But anonymity also creates risks:
* Reduced accountability: Pseudonymity can lower social costs for abusive behaviour, enabling harassment, doxxing, or coordinated attacks.
* Mob behaviour and brigading: Groups can coordinate to flood a thread, downvote or harass a target, or push a narrative that drowns out other voices.
* False personas and manipulation: Accounts may impersonate others, spread doctored content, or present false support to gain trust and later exploit it.
Key safety tools and settings (what to teach)
* Blocking and muting: Users can block or mute accounts to stop receiving direct replies or private messages.
* Reporting: Use the Report function on posts, comments or messages that violate subreddit or sitewide rules. Include context and links when reporting harassment or doxxing.
* NSFW controls: Reddit marks adult content as NSFW; users and subreddits can enable or disable visibility of sensitive content. Teach young people how Hide NSFW and account preferences work.
* Private messages (PMs): Reddit DMs can be used to move conversations off public threads. Show students how to review and disable messages from strangers in User Settings > Safety & privacy.
* Community rules & moderators: Before participating, check a subreddit’s sidebar or About tab for rules and moderator contacts. If moderation seems lax and the community is toxic, avoid it.
* Automoderator and filters: Many subreddits use bots to autoremove spam or flagged content. These tools are helpful but not foolproof; false negatives and positives occur.
Practical signs of risky community behaviour
* Rapid pileon: A thread where many accounts attack the same person or idea quickly — often a sign of coordinated brigading.
* Links to external harassment: Posts that encourage offsite doxxing, spamming, or targeted harassment should be reported immediately.
* Suspicious accounts: New accounts with few posts that appear suddenly to push the same message across multiple subreddits may be part of a coordinated campaign.
* Conversation moving to PMs: If someone pushes a private message to persuade or manipulate, treat it cautiously and keep a public record of concerning requests.
Ageadaptable scripts and actions
* For younger teens: “I don’t share personal details here. I’ll show you this message — let’s decide together.”
* For older teens: “I’m saving screenshots and reporting this post. I won’t engage further; can you help me notify the moderators?”
* For caregivers/teachers: “Pause and preserve. Don’t delete anything. Collect message links, usernames and timestamps, then use Report and contact subreddit moderators or site support.”
Classroom and family activities
* Subreddit check (10–15 minutes): Have students pick one subreddit they follow and read its About section and rules. Discuss whether the rules are clear, what behaviour is expected, and one change to improve safety.
* Detect the signal (15 minutes): Show anonymized examples of threads and ask groups to identify signs of mob behaviour, manipulation, or healthy discussion. Debrief on why each example is risky or constructive.
* Reporting walkthrough (10 minutes): Demonstrate how to report a post/comment and what information to include. Practice writing a short, factual report message for moderators.
When to escalate
* Doxxing or sharing private info: Immediately screenshot, preserve links, report to Reddit, and involve school officials or law enforcement if minors are targeted.
* Threats of violence or selfharm: Treat as urgent. If someone is at risk, contact local emergency services and relevant support organizations. For selfharm disclosure, follow a trusted adult or school protocol for wellbeing responses.
Guiding principles for safe participation
* Read the community rules before posting or commenting. A respectful visitor learns the norms first.
* Protect personal identifiers: avoid posting full names, school details, home addresses, or other data that could identify you in real life.
* Treat upvotes/downvotes as signals, not truths. Popularity does not equal accuracy.
* If a subreddit feels hostile or unmoderated, stop engaging and seek safer communities or moderated support groups.
Anonymity doesn’t remove consequences
Even when users are anonymous, posts can be linked across platforms, archived, or used to identify someone. Anonymity can protect privacy but does not guarantee safety — encourage cautious, respectful participation.
Quick starter checklist (shareable)
* Check subreddit rules before participating.
* Set Allow direct messages from everyone off if you want to restrict strangers.
* Block or mute abusive accounts; report violations with screenshots and links.
* Avoid sharing personally identifying details; use direct messages cautiously.
* If harassment escalates or involves minors, preserve evidence and contact moderators, school staff or authorities.
Where to go next
Use short, practicefocused lessons: run a reporting drill, review a subreddit’s moderation quality, or map how a piece of content moved across platforms. These activities build the habits needed to use Reddit safely while keeping access to its supportive communities.

Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - TikTok & Instagram — Algorithms, Trends, and Image Pressure
Why this matters
TikTok and Instagram use powerful recommendation systems and a steady flow of trends to shape what people see and how they feel. Short, highly polished videos and filtered images can speed up trends and intensify comparison: that affects mood, body image, and choices about safety. Teaching young people how algorithms work, how trends spread, and how to use privacy and screentime tools helps them stay creative and connected without sacrificing wellbeing.
How recommendation systems shape feeds (brief, actionable)
Recommendation systems pay attention to small signals — what you watch, rewatch, like, skip, or share — and then show you more of the same. That creates a feedback loop:
* The algorithm amplifies content that gets engagement, not content that’s necessarily accurate or healthy.
* Repeated exposure normalises trends quickly (styles, challenges, risky behaviours).
* Personalisation can create narrow feeds where people mostly see content that confirms or intensifies existing feelings.
What to teach about algorithms
* Signals matter: short views, repeats and comments teach the algorithm to show more of a clip.
* You can train your feed: use options like Not interested, Hide, or Don’t recommend to change what appears.
* Diversify intentionally: follow different creators (news, hobbies, local community) to broaden recommendations.
Trends and challenges: creative energy vs. risk
Trends are not all bad — they can spark creativity, learning and belonging. But some trends encourage risky stunts, harassment or rapid oversharing.
* Evaluate trends with three checks: Is it safe? Is it legal? Would a trusted adult or a future employer approve?
* Pause before participating in a challenge. If it asks for personal info, money, or sexualised content, don’t do it and report it.
Pause before you copy a trend
Trends spread fast. If a challenge asks for risky stunts, private content, or personal data, stop and check with a trusted adult. Doing a trend once can create a permanent online record.
Filters, edits and image pressure
Filters and editing tools change appearance and context. Even short clips can be retouched to create unrealistic standards.
* Understand the effect: filters can change skin, body shape, lighting and background; combined with careful editing, they create a polished highlight reel that isn’t a reliable picture of everyday life.
* Teach media literacy: ask "What did they leave out?" and "What does this make me compare myself to?" when viewing posts.
* Encourage authenticity experiments: creators can post a short behindthescenes clip or add captions that explain edits.
Follower counts, likes and the feedback loop
Visible metrics can drive behaviour: chasing likes or followers changes what people post and how they feel about themselves.
* Use platform options to reduce pressure: Instagram allows hiding like counts on posts; on both apps, consider limiting who can comment or message.
* Reframe success: focus conversations on creative goals, skills learned, or community impact instead of raw numbers.
Practical privacy and interaction settings (what to check now)
* Account visibility: Set accounts to Private if you want to control who follows you.
* Interactions: Limit who can comment, duet, stitch, remix or send DMs (Everyone, Friends, No one or similar options).
* Duet/Remix/Stitch controls: Turn these off or restrict to Friends if you don’t want others reusing your content.
* Location & metadata: Turn off automatic location tagging and avoid posting content that reveals daily routines or exact places.
* Sensitive content controls: Enable any platform options that reduce exposure to graphic or ageinappropriate material.
Reporting, blocking and preserving evidence
* Block and mute first for smallscale harassment; report content that violates platform rules.
* Save evidence (screenshots, profile links, timestamps) if harassment, grooming or illegal content is involved before you block or report.
* Use the app’s builtin report flows and follow up if you don’t see action; escalate to a caregiver, school staff or law enforcement when safety is at risk.
Screentime and wellbeing tactics that work
* Use builtin limits: set app timers or use Take a break reminders to interrupt long scrolling sessions.
* Curate a content diet: pick three accounts that make you feel good and intentionally follow them; unfollow or mute accounts that trigger negative comparisons.
* Schedule techfree times: bedtime, family meals, and the first 30 minutes after waking are good candidates.
* Notification hygiene: turn off push notifications for nonessential accounts and mute sensitive threads.
Short scripts to use right away
* If someone pressures you to join a risky challenge: “No thanks — I’m not comfortable with that.”
* If a creator’s content makes you feel bad: “I’m going to mute this account for a while.”
* For caregivers starting a conversation: “Show me an account you like. What do you like about it? Is there anything that makes you uncomfortable?”
Classroom and family activities (quick, practical)
* Feed audit (15 minutes): Each student lists three accounts they follow and why. For one account, check whether it makes them feel inspired, neutral, or anxious. Discuss strategies to rebalance the feed.
* Trend evaluation roleplay (15 minutes): Students work in small groups to evaluate a hypothetical challenge using safety checks (safe/legal/consent). Each group presents their decision.
* Filter transparency project (20 minutes): Ask students to create a short clip showing the original and the edited version, then caption what changed and why.
* Twoweek experiment: Hide like counts or mute one account for two weeks and reflect on mood and posting behaviour afterwards.
Quick checklist — what to do today
* Review and update privacy settings (Private account, comment limits, DM controls).
* Turn off Duet/Remix/Stitch (or restrict to friends) if you don’t want your content reused.
* Set an app timer or schedule one techfree routine each day.
* Try one feed adjustment: mute or unfollow an account that creates comparison stress and follow one new, diverse creator.
Start with curiosity, not criticism
Ask young people which creators they admire and why. When conversations begin from interest, they’re more open to discussing risks, privacy settings and how trends affect how they feel.
Wrap up — balancing creativity and care
TikTok and Instagram are powerful spaces for connection and selfexpression. Teaching how algorithms work, how trends spread, and how filters can affect selfimage gives students practical ways to protect their privacy and mental health while still enjoying creative play. Small changes — a privacy tweak, a hidden like count, or a twoweek experiment — can reduce pressure and make social media more sustainable.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Telegram & Encrypted Messaging — Speed and Privacy Tradeoffs
Why this matters
Encrypted messaging apps and fast, largescale chat tools change how people communicate: they can protect privacy and enable honest conversation, but they also make it easier for harmful content to spread quickly and for harmful behaviour to hide from moderation. Teaching the differences between encryption modes, how groups and channels amplify content, and which settings reduce risk helps students, caregivers and educators make safer choices.
How these apps are organised (clear basics)
* Private 1:1 chats: Direct conversations between two people. Some apps offer endtoend encryption (E2EE) here by default or as an option.
* Group chats: Multiparticipant spaces where messages are visible to everyone in the group. Large groups can act like public forums.
* Channels/broadcasts: Onetomany feeds where admins post and followers read. Channels often allow anonymous subscribing and can reach broad audiences quickly.
* Bots and thirdparty integrations: Automated accounts that can post content, collect data or run polls. Bots may have wide permissions if added by group admins.
* Invite links and forwarding: Links or forwarding features let messages and entire invite chains spread beyond the original circle.
Encryption and moderation — the tradeoffs
* Endtoend encryption (E2EE) means only the participants can read messages; the platform cannot decrypt them. This protects privacy but also prevents the platform from scanning content for abuse or illegal material.
* Cloudstored chats that are encrypted at rest on the provider’s servers allow platform access for backups, search, and moderation — useful for recovering accounts but less private.
* Secret or selfdestructing chats offer ephemeral messages, but screenshots, forwarded copies, or device backups can still preserve content.
Practical privacy settings and account hygiene (what to check now)
* Account identity: Use a username when possible so your phone number doesn’t have to be public. Check Privacy & Security to control who can find you by phone number.
* Message forwarding: Disable Allow message forwarding (or similar) where available to reduce unwanted spread of your messages.
* Profile and last seen: Set Last Seen and Profile Photo visibility to My Contacts or Nobody to limit whom strangers can learn about your activity.
* Group invite controls: Restrict who can add new members and who can create invite links. Prefer invite links with expirations and limited uses.
* Twostep verification / passcode: Enable a separate passcode for the app and use twofactor authentication for the associated account when supported.
* Media settings: Turn off Autodownload of media, disable Save to Gallery for received files, and consider disabling link previews in chats.
* Bot permissions: Limit the ability of bots to read messages or post on your behalf; only add trusted bots and review their permissions.
Common risks and warning signs
* Rapid forwarding and virality: A single post in a large channel can be forwarded to many groups in minutes, spreading jokes, hoaxes, or harmful media.
* Anonymous or throwaway accounts: Because it's easy to create new accounts, harassment campaigns, doxxing and coordinated manipulation are harder to trace.
* Unmoderated channels and groups: Large groups without active moderation can normalise harmful content and recruit members into closed networks.
* Disappearing content false safety: Ephemeral messages feel private but can be screenshotted or recorded; they also make evidence collection harder if abuse occurs.
* Encrypted spaces for abuse: E2EE prevents platform detection of grooming, sexual exploitation, or trade in illegal material — making it crucial to watch for behavioural signals rather than relying on automated moderation.
Short, ageadaptable scripts to use right away
* Child (young): “I won’t talk to or send photos to people I don’t know. I’ll show a grownup if something seems strange.”
* Tween: “That group seems big and I don’t recognise the people. I’m not joining without checking with a parent/teacher.”
* Teen: “I’ll save screenshots and block the user. Don’t invite me to a private group that asks for pictures or money.”
* Caregiver/teacher: “Pause — don’t delete anything. Take screenshots, note usernames and timestamps, and we’ll decide whether to report or contact authorities.”
Classroom and family activities (short, practical)
* Group/Channel Audit (15 minutes): Each student lists three groups or channels they know, identifies who can join, who moderates them, and whether invite links are public. Pick one safety change to recommend.
* Forwarding drill (10 minutes): Demonstrate how a message is forwarded and discuss how fast content can spread. Practice the rule: Pause before forwarding — check source and consent.
* Settings walkthrough (20 minutes): On a demo account, guide students through Privacy & Security settings: show how to change Last Seen, disable message previews, and enable twostep verification.
* Role play (15 minutes): One student pushes to move a chat to a private group and requests photos or money. Others practise refusing, saving evidence and telling a trusted adult.
Incident checklist — step by step
* Pause: Don’t delete messages — deletion can remove evidence.
* Preserve: Take screenshots, copy message links, note usernames, group/channel names and timestamps. Export chat if the app allows.
* Block: Stop the harasser from contacting the victim while preserving evidence.
* Report: Use the app’s Report flow for abusive or illegal content. Include context and links.
* Escalate: If minors are involved, or if the content involves exploitation, sexual abuse, threats or criminal activity, contact school authorities and local law enforcement immediately.
* Support: Offer emotional support, explain next steps, and connect the person affected with counselling or trusted adults.
When encryption limits recourse
If messages are E2EE and the platform cannot view them, platform support may still act on reported accounts, invite links, or public channel behaviour. Capture as much contextual evidence as possible (public channel posts, invite links, screenshots of profiles) and involve authorities when criminal behaviour is suspected.
Quick starter checklist (do this today)
* Turn off Allow message forwarding if available.
* Set Last Seen and Profile Photo to contacts only or nobody.
* Create and enable a separate app passcode and twostep verification.
* Disable Autodownload of media and Save to Gallery for received files.
* Review group invite settings and require admin approval for new members.
* Teach the Pause, Preserve, Report routine to all family/class members.
Encryption is not a blanket guarantee of safety
Endtoend encryption protects message content from providers, but it does not stop screenshots, recordings, or offline harms. Also, encrypted spaces can hide abusive behaviour from moderators — so behavioural signals and careful evidencecollection matter more than ever.
Final notes — balancing privacy and safety
Encrypted messaging gives people important privacy tools, but privacy and safety need to be balanced. Use privacy settings to reduce unwanted exposure, teach everyone to preserve evidence, and build clear escalation paths for when content crosses into harassment, exploitation or criminal behaviour. Small routines — a settings check, a forwarding pause, and a plan to tell a trusted adult — make large, fast networks far safer for young people.

When collecting screenshots or message links for reports, keep them private and share only with the trusted adult, school staff, or platform support handling the incident. Avoid reposting private content publicly.

Use the table as a quick reference and the conversation prompts as starters — not scripts to memorize. The goal is to open dialogue with curiosity, help young people practise specific actions (change a privacy toggle, enable 2FA), and create habits so safety becomes routine.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Understanding Cyberbullying
Why this lesson matters
Cyberbullying changes the shape of harm: it can be persistent, highly visible, and amplified beyond the immediate social circle. Young people who experience online harassment often feel isolated, embarrassed, or afraid to ask for help. This lesson gives clear language for different kinds of online abuse, helps distinguish bullying from ordinary conflict, and provides practical classroom activities and scripts so pupils, bystanders and adults can respond constructively and safely.
Key terms (short, usable definitions)
* Harassment: Repeated or targeted hostile messages intended to intimidate, humiliate or distress someone.
* Doxxing: Publishing someone’s private or identifying information (home address, school, phone number) to threaten or shame them.
* Exclusion: Deliberately leaving someone out of groups, chats or activities online to isolate them socially.
* Pileon (or mobbing): Large numbers of people attacking a person or idea at once, often by reposting, mass commenting or coordinated harassment.
* Impersonation: Creating an account that pretends to be someone else to spread rumours, damage reputation, or manipulate others.
* Cyberstalking: Persistent, unwanted attention or monitoring online that causes fear or interruption of daily life.
Bullying vs. ordinary conflict — a quick way to tell the difference
Not every argument or mean comment is bullying. Use three simple lenses:
* Intent and impact: Conflict may be mutual and situational. Bullying is intended to hurt or control and causes ongoing harm.
* Power imbalance: Bullying usually involves an imbalance of power (popularity, group size, access to private content, technical skill) that makes it hard for the target to stop the behaviour.
* Repetition and persistence: Oneoff insults differ from repeated attacks, sustained exclusion, or a pattern of targeting across platforms.
If you answer “yes” to two or more of these prompts, treat the incident as bullying: Was it intended to harm? Is there a power difference? Has it happened more than once or continued after asking to stop?
Short, realistic scenarios (use these for discussion or role play)
* The Group Chat Exclusion
* Scenario: A class group chat removes one student’s messages and shares inside jokes that deliberately leave them out. Several members mock that student in a private channel.
* Discussion prompts: Who has power in this group? How might exclusion feel different online than in person? What could a bystander do right away?
* The Viral Screenshot
* Scenario: A private photo is screenshotted and posted publicly with mocking captions. It spreads quickly and attracts mean comments.
* Discussion prompts: What steps should the target and an adult take to preserve evidence? How can the community support someone whose image has been made public?
* The Fake Account
* Scenario: Someone creates an account pretending to be a student and posts false rumours. Other students react and repost the allegations.
* Discussion prompts: How do we verify authenticity? When is it appropriate to involve school staff or platform support?
Ageadaptable response scripts (short and practical)
* For younger children (5–10): “I don’t like that. Please stop. I’ll tell an adult.”
* For tweens (11–13): “That’s not OK. Don’t share that. I’m saving this and I’ll talk to a teacher/parent.”
* For teens (14–17): “I’m not engaging further. I’ll screenshot the messages, block the account, and report it to the platform — want me to include you when I tell an adult?”
* For adults/caregivers/teachers: “Pause — do not delete anything. Preserve screenshots, note timestamps and user information, and follow the school’s reporting protocol. We’ll support the student while we investigate.”
Practical stepbystep checklist (what to do when bullying happens)
* Ensure immediate safety: If there is a threat of physical harm, contact authorities and school leadership immediately.
* Preserve evidence: Take screenshots of posts, comments and user profiles; record URLs, timestamps and any shared copies.
* Avoid retaliation: Encourage the target not to reply publicly; engaging often escalates pileons.
* Limit contact: Block or mute the harasser on the platform and secure the target’s account (password change, privacy review).
* Report: Use the platform’s report tools for harassment, impersonation or doxxing and follow school reporting protocols.
* Support: Connect the target to a trusted adult, counsellor or peersupport network and consider temporary accommodations (changes to groups, schedules) while the situation is addressed.
* Follow up: Document actions taken and outcomes, and review whether further escalation (law enforcement, platform legal team) is required.
When to escalate to authorities or specialist support
* Threats of violence, extortion, or any suggestion that the target’s physical safety is at risk.
* Sexual exploitation, images of minors distributed without consent, or signs of grooming.
* Persistent doxxing that exposes home addresses, school locations, or other highly sensitive data.
Immediate safety concerns
If a student receives a credible threat or materials that suggest sexual exploitation, involve school leadership and local law enforcement right away. Preserve all evidence and do not attempt to negotiate with the harasser alone.
Classroom activities to build skills and empathy
* Scenario role play (20 minutes): Use one of the scenarios above. Assign roles (target, bystander, perpetrator, adult) and practise a constructive response. Debrief: what felt helpful and what didn’t?
* Bystander ladder (15 minutes): Students list escalating bystander actions from loweffort (mute, don’t engage) to higheffort (save evidence, report, publicly support the target). Discuss which actions are safest in different contexts.
* Evidence preservation drill (10 minutes): Demonstrate how to take screenshots, copy message links and record timestamps on several platforms using mock content.
* Empathy mapping (15 minutes): For a recent incident (anonymized), map what the target might feel, think, see and need. Use the map to plan supportive classroom language and actions.
Restorative approaches vs. punitive responses
Restorative practices focus on repair: bringing harmed parties together (when safe), acknowledging impact, and agreeing on steps to make amends. They work best when the harasser accepts responsibility and the target agrees to participate.
Punitive responses (suspensions, bans) may be necessary when safety is at risk, when the behaviour is repeated, or when the offender refuses to engage in repair. Best practice: pair accountability with learning — require reflection, mediation or restorative tasks where appropriate.
A brief restorative sequence
* Private intake: Hear the target’s needs and consent before any meeting.
* Facilitated meeting: With trained staff, the harmed person explains impact; the harasser listens and answers clarifying questions.
* Agreement: The harasser acknowledges harm and commits to specific reparative actions (apology, community service, digital conduct plan).
* Follow up: Check progress and safety; adjust the agreement if necessary.
Start by teaching small, specific actions
Practice a short bystander script in class: “Stop. I don’t think that’s OK. Are you OK? I can help you tell an adult.” Small rehearsed responses increase the chance students will act when it matters.
Assessment and reflection prompts (use for exit tickets or class discussion)
* What made this example bullying rather than a disagreement? Name two signals you used to decide.
* If you saw a peer being mocked in a public post, what are three safe things you could do in the next five minutes?
* How would you support someone whose private photo was shared without permission? Who would you tell and why?
Closing — building a classroom culture that reduces harm
Consistent language, short rehearsed scripts, and clear reporting routines make it easier for students to speak up. Combine prevention (digital citizenship lessons, privacy skills, group norms) with clear support (trusted adults, reporting paths, restorative options). Regularly revisit scenarios and check that students know how to preserve evidence and where to go when safety is at risk.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Responding to Bullying: Steps & Scripts
Why this lesson matters
Bullying online can escalate quickly and feel overwhelming. Clear, practiced steps help targets, bystanders and adults act calmly, preserve evidence, and get the right people involved. This lesson gives a practical response plan, readytouse scripts for different ages, and guidance on when to involve platforms, schools or authorities.
A simple response flow (what to do, in order)
* Pause and breathe — don’t reply. Immediate reactions often escalate situations or erase evidence.
* Preserve evidence — save screenshots, message links, usernames and timestamps. Capture public posts, comments and direct messages before anything is deleted.
* Protect the target — block or mute the harasser, tighten account privacy, and change passwords if account takeover is suspected.
* Report to the platform — use the app/site Report tools and include context and links so moderators can act.
* Tell a trusted adult or school staff — share evidence and agree next steps (support, accommodation, or escalation).
* Escalate if needed — if threats, sexual exploitation, doxxing, or stalking are involved, contact law enforcement or child protection services.
* Follow up — check that reports were received, confirm any school actions, and offer ongoing emotional support to the target.
Preserving evidence: quick howtos
* Screenshots: Capture full screens showing usernames, message text, and timestamps. On mobile, take multiple screenshots if the conversation is long.
* Message links: Copy or export message links (where supported). In browsers, copy the page URL and note the time and browser used.
* Profiles and posts: Save the offending account’s profile page, any relevant posts and comment threads. Use Print to PDF or export options when available.
* Don’t delete original content unless advised by investigators. Deleting can remove context or make it harder for platforms and authorities to act.
Preserve evidence — don’t edit or crop it
When preparing evidence, keep the original images and files intact. Cropping or altering screenshots can raise doubts during investigations. Store evidence privately and share only with trusted adults, school staff or platform support.
Practical reporting tips for platforms
* Use the platform’s Report button for harassment, impersonation or doxxing. Attach screenshots and precise timestamps when prompted.
* For impersonation, include links to the real account (if available) and a short description of how the fake account is causing harm.
* When reporting sexual content involving minors or grooming, choose the platform options that signal child sexual exploitation so the report is fasttracked.
* Keep a record of report confirmation numbers, case IDs or support emails so you can follow up if action isn’t taken.
When to involve school staff vs. law enforcement
* Involve school staff when incidents involve students, occur on school networks or affect school life (classes, clubs, sports). Schools can investigate, apply restorative or disciplinary measures, and provide support.
* Involve law enforcement immediately when there are credible threats of physical harm, extortion, persistent stalking, or distribution of sexual images of minors. If unsure, consult your school’s designated safeguarding lead for guidance.
Ageadaptable scripts (short, easy to use)
Child (5–10)
* To say no and get help: “I don’t like that. Please stop. I’ll tell an adult.”
* If pressured: Show the message to an adult right away and say, “This scares me.”
Tween (11–13)
* To respond to a message that feels wrong: “I’m not comfortable with this. I’m going to save it and talk to a parent/teacher.”
* To a peer who’s sharing a private photo: “Please don’t share that. It’s private and could hurt them.”
Teen (14–17)
* To refuse engagement: “I won’t reply. I’m saving screenshots, blocking the account, and reporting it.”
* To a friend who’s been targeted: “I’ve got your back. Take screenshots, don’t delete anything, and let’s tell the counselor together.”
Caregiver/Teacher (how to talk to a young person)
* Immediate comfort: “You’re safe now. Thank you for telling me. We’ll keep the evidence and decide what to do next together.”
* To a teen who worries about consequences: “We’ll protect your privacy as much as possible. The priority is your safety; we’ll handle the rest carefully.”
What to say to school administrators (sample message)
Short email template to report an incident:
Subject: Urgent — online harassment involving [student name]
Hello [Name],
I need to report an incident affecting [student name/class]. A [description: e.g., fake account/doxxing/harassing messages] occurred on [platform] on [date/time]. I have screenshots and message links attached. The student is [brief note on immediate safety]. Please advise on next steps and any documentation you need. We’d appreciate guidance on supports and any disciplinary process.
Thank you, [Your name & role]
Practical guidance on blocking, privacy and account safety
* Block and mute first to stop contact. Then preserve evidence before removing access.
* Turn accounts to Private, remove unknown followers/friends, and check Connected apps or Authorized devices for suspicious access.
* Change passwords and enable twofactor authentication for accounts that appear compromised.
Restorative vs punitive responses (short primer)
* Restorative approaches focus on repairing harm: facilitated conversations, apologies, and commitments to change. They work best when the harasser accepts responsibility and the harmed person consents to the process.
* Punitive actions (suspension, bans, legal complaints) are necessary when safety is at risk, when behaviour is repeated, or when the offender refuses to take responsibility.
* Best practice: combine accountability with education (reflection tasks, counselling) and ensure the target’s safety and wishes guide the outcome.
Classroom activity to rehearse responses (20 minutes)
* Present a short anonymized scenario (e.g., a private photo shared publicly).
* In small groups, students write a 3step response plan using the flow above (Pause, Preserve, Protect, Report).
* Roleplay using the ageappropriate scripts: one student acts as the target, one as a bystander, one as an adult.
* Debrief: what was helpful, what felt hard, and what support would they want in real life.
Quick incident checklist (printable)
* Pause — don’t reply.
* Save screenshots and message links; note usernames and timestamps.
* Block/mute the harasser; secure the account.
* Report to the platform; keep report IDs.
* Tell school staff or a trusted adult; share evidence.
* Escalate to police if there are threats, sexual exploitation of minors, or doxxing.
* Follow up: check report outcomes and provide ongoing support.
Practice the plan before you need it
Run a short drill once a term: practise saving a screenshot, writing a report, and sending the sample message to school staff. Familiarity reduces panic and speeds effective action.
Closing
Responding to online bullying is a team effort: a calm first response, careful evidencecollection, timely reporting, and compassionate support make the biggest difference. Use the scripts and checklists here to make those steps routine so students and families can act quickly and safely when it matters most.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - NSFW / NSFL Exposure: Immediate and Ongoing Responses
Why this matters
Exposure to sexual or extremely violent content (NSFW/NSFL) can be accidental — a misdirected link, an algorithmic recommendation, or a forwarded message — or malicious, as when someone shares material to shock, humiliate or groom. Such exposure can be distressing, confusing, and sometimes dangerous (especially when minors are involved). Clear, calm procedures help protect safety, preserve evidence, and support emotional recovery.
How young people commonly encounter NSFW/NSFL material
* Algorithmic recommendations or autoplay that surface graphic clips.
* Shared links or forwarded messages in group chats and channels.
* Ingame or platform usergenerated content (images, videos, mods).
* Direct messages from strangers or acquaintances asking to view or exchange explicit content.
* Search results, browser suggestions, or accidentally loaded webpages.
Immediate steps for young people (what to do right away)
* Pause: Stop scrolling. Close or back out of the app or tab to limit further exposure.
* Don’t share: Do not forward, download, screenshot, or repost the content.
* Tell a trusted adult: Show an adult the message or describe where it came from. If you can’t show it safely, tell them the platform, username, time and what you saw.
Short scripts kids can use right away
* “That made me uncomfortable. I’m not sharing it. I’m going to tell an adult.”
* “I didn’t want to see that. Please don’t send content like this to me.”
What adults should do immediately (careful, factual actions)
* Ensure immediate emotional safety: Move the young person from the device, offer water and a calm space, and use validating language (see below).
* Preserve evidence: If the content is relevant to reporting, save it exactly as received — do not crop, edit, or annotate images. Capture fullscreen screenshots that include usernames, timestamps and URLs, or use builtin export features if the platform provides them.
* Limit further spread: Ask everyone involved not to forward or repost. If a group or server is involved, remove the young person from the space temporarily.
* Report promptly: Use the platform’s Report flow and include context, links and screenshots. For school networks, follow internal reporting protocols.
Do not redistribute or publish the content
Sharing or reposting graphic material — even to raise alarm — multiplies harm and can be illegal. Keep evidence private and share it only with people who must see it (platform support, school safeguarding lead, law enforcement).
Preserving evidence — practical guidance
* Keep originals intact: Save the original files or screenshots without editing. Store them in a private, secure location (not on public drives or social posts).
* Capture contextual details: Record the platform, usernames, group or channel names, full URLs, and precise timestamps (including time zone if known).
* Export chat logs if available: Some apps let you export conversations or message links — use these features when possible.
* Note devices and accounts: Record which device was used, whether the account was logged in, and any recent account changes.
When to escalate to authorities or specialist services
Escalate immediately when content involves:
* Sexual images or sexual activity involving minors (this is child sexual exploitation and must be reported to law enforcement and child protection services).
* Threats of violence, extortion, or clear criminal activity.
* Persistent harassment that endangers the young person’s wellbeing.
If unsure, consult your school’s designated safeguarding lead or local child protection authorities — err on the side of safety.
Reporting and contentremoval steps (platforms & followup)
* Use the inapp Report button and choose the option closest to the issue (sexual content, exploitation, violent content). Attach screenshots and links.
* If reporting options are limited, find the platform’s safety center or abuse email and send a concise message with evidence and context.
* Request removal and a case/reference number. Save confirmation emails or support ticket IDs for follow up.
* For crossplatform spread, report the content on each service where it appears and ask support teams to coordinate takedown if possible.
* If content involves minors, mention this clearly in the report so platforms fasttrack the case and engage their child safety teams.
Practical templates for removal requests (short, factual)
* To platform support: “Content involving [brief description] was posted by [username/link] on [date/time]. It includes sexual images of a minor / graphic violent content. I request removal and a reference number. Evidence attached.”
Supporting emotional recovery (deescalation & validation)
* Validate feelings: “I’m sorry you saw this. That must be upsetting.” Avoid minimizing: don’t say “You’ll get over it.”
* Grounding techniques: encourage slow breathing, name things in the room (5 things you can see), or use a short walk to reset.
* Normalize helpseeking: “This is not your fault. Let’s get help and decide next steps together.”
* Avoid reexposure: Don’t force reviewing images to assess harm; evidence preservation should be done by the adult if needed.
When to offer professional help
If the young person shows prolonged distress, nightmares, withdrawal, selfharm talk, or worsening school performance, connect them with a counsellor, mental health professional, or childprotection services as appropriate.
Privacy, confidentiality and who should know
* Limit knowledge to those who need to act (caregiver, safeguarding lead, designated staff, platform support, law enforcement).
* Keep conversations factual and private; avoid discussing details in public or with unrelated peers.
* Document who was informed and what actions were taken for accountability and followup.
Prevention and longterm followup
* Review and tighten account settings: disable autodownload of media, restrict who can send messages, and remove unknown contacts.
* Teach the Pause — Don’t Share — Tell routine as a short, memorable habit.
* Run a short digitalsafety refresher: how to report, how algorithms surface content, and how to curate feeds.
* Monitor for retaliation or continued harassment and update safety plans if needed.
Classroom and family activities (practice makes calmer responses)
* Quick drill (10 minutes): Practise the Pause — Don’t Share — Tell routine with mock notifications and decide who to tell in the household/school.
* Evidence preservation demo (15 minutes): On a demo account, show how to capture a full screenshot, copy message links and export chat logs without editing files.
* Emotional first aid (15 minutes): Teach simple grounding and breathing exercises; roleplay comforting language and appropriate referral phrases.
Short checklists (printable)
If a young person sees NSFW/NSFL content:
* Pause — close the app/tab.
* Don’t share, forward or screenshot the content yourself.
* Tell a trusted adult and describe where it came from.
For adults responding right away:
* Ensure the young person is safe and calm.
* Preserve evidence (full screenshots, URLs, timestamps) without editing.
* Report to the platform and follow school safeguarding protocols.
* Offer emotional support and connect to professional help if needed.
Start with validation
A simple first line — “I’m sorry you had to see that” — helps the young person feel heard and safe, which makes practical steps (preserving evidence, reporting) easier to do.
Closing
Handling NSFW/NSFL exposure blends technical steps (preserving evidence, reporting, removing content) with human care (validation, grounding, privacy). Practise short routines, keep evidence secure, and prioritise the young person’s wellbeing. When in doubt about legality or child safety, escalate quickly to the designated safeguarding lead or local authorities.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Online Experiences & Mental Health
Why this matters
Online life and mental health are tightly linked. What young people see, how long they scroll, and the social comparisons they encounter can affect sleep, anxiety, selfimage and mood. Helping students recognise these links, build simple protective routines, and know when to seek extra support reduces harm and strengthens resilience.
How online habits connect to wellbeing
Sleep: Latenight scrolling and bright screens delay sleep onset and reduce sleep quality. Even short interruptions (notifications) fragment rest and make concentration harder the next day.
Anxiety and mood: Constant comparison, negative comments, or unpredictable feeds can increase worry, low mood and social stress. Algorithms that push sensational content can intensify fear or hopelessness.
Body image and selfesteem: Filtered, staged content models narrow ideals. Repeated exposure to highly edited portrayals of bodies and lifestyles shifts what students perceive as "normal."
FOMO and social pressure: Seeing curated highlights from peers can create fear of missing out and pressure to be constantly available or to perform online.
Doomscrolling: Repeated consumption of negative news or upsetting clips increases stress and can lead to helplessness or avoidance of helpful activities.
Simple language to use with students
* "What you see online affects how you feel — both right away and the next day."
* "Algorithms try to keep you engaged, not necessarily to make you feel good."
* "Comparing your whole life to a few edited clips isn’t fair to you."
Practical protective routines (easy to adopt)
Start small and specific. Routines that are short and repeatable stick.
* Evening tech curfew: No screens 30–60 minutes before bedtime; charge devices outside the bedroom.
* Notification hygiene: Turn off nonessential push notifications or enable Do Not Disturb during focused times and sleep.
* Scheduled breaks: Use short, timed breaks (5–10 minutes every hour) that include movement or a breathing exercise to interrupt long sessions of scrolling.
* One app swap: Replace 10–15 minutes of passive browsing with a short offline activity (drawing, walk, chat with a friend) each day.
* Feed refresh: Follow three accounts that make you feel inspired or calm; mute or unfollow accounts that trigger negative comparisons.
Offline recovery activities
* Microbreaks: 3–5 minutes of stretching, stepping outside, or mindful breathing.
* Creative reset: Sketch, journal a quick gratitude note, or play a short song instrument—activities that redirect attention and create accomplishment.
* Social checkins: Call or sit with a friend or family member for a few minutes to ground social perspective outside the app.
Classroom practices to build resilience
* Start lessons with a 2minute grounding (deep breaths, noticing five senses). Small routines lower reactivity and improve focus.
* Run a weekly "feed audit" where students check one app, note one post that helped and one that hurt, and adjust following/muting choices.
* Teach algorithm literacy: a 10minute demo showing how engagement affects recommendations and how to use "Not interested" or hiding features.
* Create a classroom wellbeing contract: agreed tech curfew, designated devicefree times, and a plan for when someone feels overwhelmed.
Activities you can use (15–30 minutes)
* Twoweek experiment: Students pick one habit (e.g., no phones at dinner or hiding like counts) and journal mood changes. Compare reflections after two weeks.
* Mood + Media map: Students keep a simple log for a week — app used, time spent, and mood before/after. Use anonymized data to discuss patterns and options.
* Filter transparency: Students produce one original photo and one lightly edited version, then caption what changed and why. Discuss media literacy and selfimage.
Scripts for quick conversations
With a student who seems anxious or withdrawn:
* "I’ve noticed you seem more tired/anxious lately. Have you noticed any online things that change how you feel?"
* "Would you like to try a oneweek experiment to see if changing one habit helps? I can support you with it."
With a teen resistant to limits:
* "Let’s try a twoweek experiment. If you track how you feel and we review the results together, we’ll decide what to do next."
When to be concerned — signs that professional help may be needed
Look for changes that persist or worsen, such as:
* Persistent sleep loss affecting school performance or daily functioning.
* Marked withdrawal from friends and previously enjoyed activities.
* Intense, persistent anxiety or panic attacks tied to online interactions.
* Severe negative body image, disordered eating behaviours, or talk of selfharm.
* Repeated exposure to traumatic content with ongoing distress, nightmares or avoidance.
If any of these signs appear, follow school safeguarding protocols and consider referral to counselling, mental health services, or pediatric care. When safety is at risk (selfharm intent, suicidal talk, or threats), escalate immediately to emergency services and designated safeguarding leads.
Practical referral checklist for educators and caregivers
* Document observations: note behaviours, dates, and any relevant online material (do not copy graphic content unless required for authorities).
* Have a calm checkin: use validating language ("I’m sorry you’re feeling this way") and ask direct questions about safety.
* Share concerns with the school counsellor or mental health lead and agree next steps with the family.
* If urgent, contact emergency services or crisis lines and follow local protocols.
Begin with curiosity and collaboration
Ask about platforms and feelings rather than demand device access. Codesigning a short experiment (two weeks) with the student increases buyin and produces useful data for next steps.
Watch for immediate safety signs
If a student expresses suicidal thoughts, talks about selfharm, or shows signs of severe decline, act at once: stay with the student, contact your school’s safeguarding lead, and call emergency services if the risk is immediate.
Quick checklists to share with families (one page)
* Tonight: turn off screens 30–60 minutes before bedtime and enable Do Not Disturb.
* This week: pick one app to mute/unfollow if it causes stress; add one uplifting account to follow.
* Ongoing: schedule a weekly 5minute checkin about online stress; practise a 2minute grounding at bedtime.
Closing — making small changes that add up
Digital wellbeing is not about forbidding devices; it’s about noticing patterns and building simple, repeatable habits that protect sleep, mood and selfimage. Short experiments, grounded conversations and clear referral steps make it easier to support students — and to help them grow into resilient, reflective digital citizens.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Creating Supportive Peer Cultures
Why this matters
Peer culture shapes daytoday experiences more than rules alone. When classmates step in, support targets, and repair harm, online and offline spaces become safer and more welcoming. Teaching specific bystander behaviours, offering short support scripts, and using restorative responses when appropriate builds empathy, accountability and trust.
Principles for supportive peer cultures
* Make safety social: normalise small, safe interventions so students see helping as a shared value, not a heroic exception.
* Teach doable actions: short, rehearsed steps are more likely to be used in the moment than vague advice.
* Protect targets and bystanders: prioritise safety and privacy; never require public confrontation or exposure.
* Couple accountability with learning: hold people responsible while giving space for repair and growth when it’s safe and appropriate.
Promoting bystander intervention — the "Bystander Ladder"
Provide a range of options from low to high effort so students can act in ways that match their comfort and context:
* Notice and name: Quietly say, "That sounds hurtful," to signal the behaviour is unacceptable.
* Distract or redirect: Change the subject, start a new activity, or post a neutral comment to defuse attention.
* Check in privately: Message or pull aside the person being targeted: "Are you okay? Want me to stay with you?"
* Support publicly: Post a short, positive comment that redirects support to the target ("We don’t do that here — you’re not alone.").
* Save evidence and report: Screenshot or copy links, and report the behaviour to a trusted adult or platform moderators.
* Escalate: If threats, sexual exploitation or doxxing are involved, inform school staff or law enforcement immediately.
Short, ageadaptable bystander scripts
* Children (5–10): “Stop — that’s not kind. Are you okay? Let’s tell a teacher.”
* Tweens (11–13): “Hey, that comment isn’t cool. I’m going to check that person’s OK and tell an adult if needed.”
* Teens (14–17): “I don’t want to see this. I’ll screenshot it and report it — do you want me there when you tell the counsellor?”
Tips to increase use of bystander actions
* Rehearse weekly: run 5minute drills using short scenarios so responses are practiced, not improvised.
* Publicly recognise lowrisk helpers: acknowledge students who safely support peers to reinforce the norm.
* Provide private routes for reporting: allow anonymous or confidential reporting channels so students can escalate without fear.
Peer support scripts — what to say when someone is hurt
* Immediate comfort: “I’m sorry that happened to you. You’re not alone.”
* Practical offer: “Do you want to go tell [teacher/counsellor] with me? I can stay while you talk.”
* If they’re unsure about reporting: “We can save the messages first and decide together what to do next.”
Restorative approaches vs. punitive responses
Restorative methods focus on repair: helping the person harmed describe impact, giving the person who caused harm a chance to understand and make amends, and agreeing on concrete steps forward. Punitive measures (suspension, exclusion) may be necessary for safety or repeated harm, but are less effective alone at rebuilding relationships.
When to prioritise restorative responses
* The harmed person consents to a restorative process.
* The incident does not involve imminent physical danger, sexual exploitation of minors, or serious criminal acts.
* The person who caused harm accepts responsibility and is willing to engage.
A compact restorative sequence (practical steps)
* Private intake: Meet independently with the harmed person and the person who caused harm to hear needs and willingness.
* Facilitated dialogue: A trained adult guides a meeting where impact is shared, questions are asked, and responsibility is acknowledged.
* Agreement on repair: Concrete actions (apology, written reflection, community service, digital conduct plan) with timelines.
* Follow up: Check progress, confirm safety, and adjust the plan if necessary.
Short restorative scripts (teacher prompts)
* Opening the conversation: “We’re here to understand what happened and how to make things right. Can each of you say what you noticed and how it made you feel?”
* For the person harmed: “What do you need to feel safer and respected?”
* For the person who caused harm: “What do you think the impact of that action was, and what would you be willing to do to repair it?”
Safety and limits
* Do not force restorative meetings when safety or power imbalances are unresolved. Use restorative options only when the harmed person feels safe and agrees to participate.
* Maintain records of decisions, agreed actions and followups for accountability.
Classroom activities to foster empathy and inclusion
Empathy Mapping (15–20 minutes)

Start with a few concrete practices (the Ladder, buddy pairs, monthly reflections) and iterate with students so the culture belongs to them as much as to adults.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Ethics and Practicalities of Monitoring
Why this lesson matters
Educators and school leaders have a duty of care to keep students safe online. At the same time, monitoring tools and filters can intrude on privacy, chill learning, or misidentify normal behaviour as risky. The goal is to protect students while respecting their dignity and rights — using tools that are proportionate, transparent, and paired with clear human decisionmaking.
Guiding principles for ethical monitoring
* Purpose first: Use monitoring only to achieve a clearly defined safety or educational purpose (e.g., preventing selfharm, blocking malicious downloads, enforcing acceptable use), not to collect broad behavioural profiles.
* Proportionality: Choose the least intrusive method that meets the safety need. Prefer networklevel protection for known threats over continual personal surveillance.
* Transparency and consent: Tell students and families what is monitored, why, how long data is kept, and who can access it. Where legal, secure informed consent and offer clear contact points for questions.
* Human review and escalation: Automated alerts should be reviewed by trained staff before action. Avoid acting on single automated flags without context or corroboration.
* Data minimisation and retention: Collect only necessary data; delete or anonymise logs after a defined period and document retention policies.
* Equity and nondiscrimination: Watch for biased algorithms or disproportionate impacts on particular groups. Regularly audit outcomes to ensure fair treatment.
Types of monitoring and their practical limits
Network filtering and web proxies
* What they do: Block known malicious domains, filter categories (adult content, gambling), and prevent unsafe downloads at the network level.
* Strengths: Low personal intrusion; protects all devices on the network.
* Limits: Can overblock educational content, and cannot see content sent over encrypted channels without additional configuration.
DNS and routerlevel protections
* What they do: Offer a first layer of domainlevel controls (safe DNS lists, parental control profiles at the router).
* Strengths: Easy to deploy for families and schools; useful for basic blocking.
* Limits: Coarse control, easily bypassed by VPNs or alternative DNS settings.
Device management and parental control apps
* What they do: Enforce app restrictions, time limits, and remote lockdowns on managed devices.
* Strengths: Useful for managed school devices and enforcing acceptable use.
* Limits: Can be invasive on personal devices; may harm trust if used without clear notice.
Classroom management software
* What they do: Let teachers view student screens, push websites, or limit browsing during class.
* Strengths: Supports focused instruction and rapid intervention during lessons.
* Limits: Screen viewing should be timelimited and only during class; constant surveillance undermines autonomy and can distract learning.
Automated content scanning & keyword alerts
* What they do: Scan messages, posts or uploads for keywords, sentiment, or image characteristics and generate alerts.
* Strengths: Helps flag urgent risks (selfharm threats, sexual exploitation) at scale.
* Limits: High false positive rates, context blindness, and the potential to stigmatise normal adolescent language. Never act on a single flag without human review.
Behavioural analytics and longterm profiling
* What they do: Build patterns of use over time to detect anomalies or risky behaviour.
* Strengths: May spot slowdeveloping concerns that single alerts miss.
* Limits: Risk of profiling, privacy invasion, and poor transparency. Use only with strict safeguards and clear, limited aims.
Practical selection checklist for tools
Before deploying any product, run through this checklist:
* Define the precise problem you want to solve.
* Can a less intrusive solution (education, peer support, oneoff checks) achieve that goal?
* Does the tool collect only what is strictly necessary?
* Who will access alerts and data? Are they trained and bound by confidentiality?
* How are false positives handled? Is there a clear human review workflow?
* What are the data retention and deletion policies? Where is data stored and who owns it?
* Does the vendor meet datasecurity standards and local legal requirements (e.g., data localisation, COPPA/GDPR implications)?
* Is there a plan for periodic auditing of outcomes and bias?
* How will you communicate the policy to students, staff and families?
* What optout or appeal processes exist for families and students?
Designing a humane implementation plan
* Cocreate policies: Involve students, teachers and families in drafting acceptableuse and monitoring policies so expectations are shared and realistic.
* Limit scope: For example, use classroom screen viewing only during lessons and not for offhours monitoring; set keyword alerts to narrow categories that truly require rapid attention.
* Train staff: Ensure adults who receive alerts know how to interpret them, preserve evidence, keep confidentiality, and escalate appropriately (mentalhealth support, safeguarding lead, law enforcement when necessary).
* Use transparency channels: Publish a short, plainlanguage privacy notice for families and a onepage student summary explaining what is monitored, why, and how to get help.
* Provide support, not punishment: Monitoring should trigger human outreach and support first — disciplinary actions should be proportionate and follow documented processes.
Templates and scripts (copy and adapt)
Oneparagraph family notice
"To keep students safe while using school resources, we monitor network activity and use classroom management tools during lessons. We will only use automated alerts to identify urgent risks and will never share personal data outside school without consent or legal obligation. If you have questions or concerns, please contact [position/name] at [email/phone]."
Short student script for class introduction
"During class I may ask you to open a site or close tabs. We also use screensharing tools during lessons so I can support learning. If anything makes you uncomfortable online, tell me or another trusted adult — we’ll help."
Parent consent checklist (for a consent form)
* Purpose of monitoring: safety / classroom management / device security
* Types of data collected: domains visited, screen snapshots in class, flagged messages
* Who can view data: designated staff only
* Retention period: e.g., 90 days for logs, 2 years for serious incidents
* Appeal contact: [role/email]
Risks to watch and mitigations
* Chilling effects: Overly broad monitoring can suppress legitimate discussion. Mitigate by minimising data collection and clearly communicating boundaries.
* False positives and harm: Automated systems may mistakenly flag normal behaviour. Require trained human review and provide a confidential appeal path for students.
* Data breach risk: Treat monitoring data as sensitive. Encrypt storage, limit access, and include log reviews of who accessed records.
* Equity harms: Monitor testing and audits to ensure some groups are not disproportionately targeted by automated rules.
When monitoring detects serious harm
* Prioritise safety: If there is a credible threat to a student’s safety (selfharm, explicit sexual exploitation involving minors, credible threats of violence), follow your safeguarding and reporting protocols immediately.
* Preserve evidence: Capture system logs, timestamps and context without altering originals; share only with authorised responders and law enforcement if needed.
* Communicate carefully: Inform families and relevant staff in a factual, compassionate way; avoid public disclosure.
Closing guidance — build trust before installing tools
Monitoring is a schoolwide responsibility that works best when paired with strong relationships, clear education about online risks, and accessible support channels. Start small, document decisions, be transparent with students and families, audit outcomes regularly, and prioritise human judgement over automated answers.
:::
Keep human judgment central
Automated tools are useful for scale, but real safety decisions should be made by trained adults who can interpret context and respond with care.
Don’t let monitoring replace teaching
Use monitoring to complement — not replace — digital citizenship education, trusted adult relationships, and restorative practices for addressing harm.

Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Gamification & Active Learning Techniques
Why use gamification and active learning?
Gamification and active learning turn abstract safety concepts into lived practice. When students play, solve, perform or compete around real scenarios, they build habits, rehearse scripts and test choices in lowrisk settings. Good designs increase engagement without sacrificing reflection or safety.
Core design principles
* Purpose first: Link every game mechanic to a learning objective (e.g., "practice reporting a harassment incident" or "recognise phishing signs"). Don’t gamify for fun alone.
* Lowstakes practice: Make failure a learning moment — puzzles and roleplays should offer feedback and a chance to try again.
* Equity and access: Design mechanics that work for diverse learners and avoid rewarding only speed or prior privilege.
* Clear rules and privacy: Explain what data is collected (points, screenshots) and keep sensitive roleplay details private.
* Reflection baked in: After play, move quickly to debriefs that surface reasoning, emotions and next steps.
Designing quizzes that teach, not just test
Quizzes can be formative practice when questions require explanation or decision reasoning.
* Use scenariobased items: present a short message or post and ask students to choose one of several realistic responses and explain why.
* Mix question types: multiple choice for quick checks, short freetext for reasoning, and confidence ratings ("How sure are you?") to surface overconfidence.
* Provide immediate, instructive feedback: explain why distractors are wrong and offer a short script students could use in that moment.
Sample quiz item
* Scenario: "You receive a DM from an account you don’t recognise that asks for a photo."
* Choices: A) Send a photo. B) Ask why and then send. C) Don’t reply and show a trusted adult. D) Share the message with a friend and ask them what to do.
* Good feedback: Explain risks of private requests, why C is safest for young children, and how older students might save evidence and report.
Designing short escape rooms (digital or physical)
Escape rooms are excellent for chaining skills: recognising a problem, preserving evidence, reporting, and supporting peers.
Steps to build a 30–45 minute escape room
* Choose a tight learning objective (e.g., "Respond to a grooming attempt").
* Create 4–6 stations or puzzles that each practise one skill (identify red flags; save screenshot & note timestamp; draft a report; roleplay a bystander script).
* Use realistic props: anonymised screenshots, mock profiles, private message logs (redacted), and printed evidence forms.
* Add branching: one puzzle’s answer changes the next station so decisions have consequences and prompt discussion.
* Debrief with a checklist: What clues led you to decide? What would you do differently next time?
Example puzzle chain
* Puzzle A: Spot the red flags in a fake DM. Correct picks reveal a code.
* Puzzle B: Use the code to open a file that contains a mock screenshot. Students must capture the screenshot correctly (full screen, timestamps visible) to get the next clue.
* Puzzle C: Draft a short report to platform support with the provided evidence.
Roleplays and scenario practice for real skills
Roleplay turns scripts into muscle memory. Keep scenes short and tightly scaffolded.
* Prepare clear roles: target, bystander, harasser, and observer. Give each a oneline brief.
* Use a simple rubric for observers: Did the actor pause? Preserve evidence? Use a deescalation script? Offer support?
* Rotate roles so everyone practices both helpseeking and intervention.
* End with a structured debrief: What felt hard? Which phrases landed well? What followup would you take in real life?
Sample roleplay rubric (quick)
* Paused and didn’t reply: yes/no
* Preserved evidence: yes/no (what evidence?)
* Used an ageappropriate script: yes/no (quote it)
* Asked for adult help when needed: yes/no
Point systems, badges and reward structures that reinforce kindness
Rewards should highlight behaviour you want more of: reporting responsibly, supporting peers, practising privacy checks.
* Points for process, not outcomes: give points for doing the right steps (saved evidence, reported, supported a peer), not just for ‘winning’ a confrontation.
* Badges as visible microcredentials: Examples — "Evidence Preserver" (saved proper screenshots), "Kind Responder" (sent three supportive messages), "Privacy Pro" (completed a settings checklist).
* Leaderboards with caution: If using leaderboards, provide multiple dimensions (collaboration points, kindness points) and optout choices to avoid shaming.
* Nonmaterial rewards: privileges (lead a class debrief), responsibilities (peer mentor), and certificates often work better than candy or trinkets.
Example badge table
Badge | Earned for | Reinforces
Evidence Preserver | Submitting complete, unedited evidence in a drill | Careful reporting habits
Kind Responder | Posting a supportive private message in a roleplay | Empathy and bystander action
Settings Checker | Completing a privacy/settings walkthrough | Technical hygiene
Avoiding common pitfalls
* Don’t overemphasise competition: Some students disengage from contests. Offer collaborative goals and team challenges.
* Don’t gamify shame: Never publicly expose mistakes. Use private feedback for errors that could embarrass.
* Don’t reward risky behaviour: Don’t give points for ‘exposing’ or calling out peers publicly — reward support and proper escalation instead.
Assessment and reflection
* Use quick exit tickets: one thing learned, one thing they’d still like to practise.
* Collect process evidence: logs of reports drafted in an escape room, roleplay rubrics, quiz reasoning answers.
* Run periodic twoweek experiments: e.g., does adding a kindness badge increase private support messages? Use simple counts and student reflections to evaluate.
Practical session templates (timeboxed)
* 20minute quiz + 10minute debrief: Formative checks and wholeclass discussion of reasoning.
* 45minute mini escape room: 30 minutes of stations + 15 minutes debrief and badge awarding.
* 30minute roleplay lab: three 7minute scenes with rotation + 9minute group reflection.
Start with lowrisk, highfeedback activities
Begin term with short quizzes and roleplays that give immediate corrective feedback. These build confidence before running larger, more immersive games like escape rooms.
Closing
When thoughtfully designed, gamification and active learning make safety skills memorable and repeatable. Link mechanics to clear learning goals, prioritise equitable engagement, build reflection into every activity, and reward the specific behaviours that keep online spaces safer and kinder.

Why use gamification and active learning?
Short, interactive activities help students practise safety skills until they feel automatic. Welldesigned games, quizzes and roleplays turn abstract rules into repeatable behaviours — and they give teachers fast diagnostic information about what to reteach.
Core design principles
* Connect mechanics to outcomes: Every point, badge or timer should map to a clear learning objective (for example: "recognise phishing signs", "save evidence correctly", or "support a peer safely").
* Lowstakes repetition: Let students fail and retry. Make consequences learning opportunities, not public punishments.
* Accessibility and equity: Design for varied reading levels, language backgrounds and neurodiversity. Offer multiple routes to succeed (speed is not the only metric).
* Protect privacy: Keep sensitive roleplay outcomes private and avoid public shaming. Obtain consent before sharing screenshots or recordings.
* Builtin reflection: After play, always debrief: what cues did you use? what felt hard? what will you do differently next time?
Designing effective quizzes
Use scenariobased items that require decisionmaking, not trivia. A good quiz question shows a short message or post and asks students to choose a next action and explain why.
* Provide immediate feedback: Explain why each option is or isn’t safe and give a short script students could use in the moment.
* Include a confidence rating: Ask "How sure are you?" to surface overconfidence and direct further practice.
Sample quiz item
Scenario: "You get a DM from an account you don’t know asking for your school email and password to help with a ‘survey.’"
Choices: A) Share the details to be helpful.
B) Ask for more info and then send if it seems okay.
C) Don’t reply, screenshot the message, and tell a trusted adult.
D) Reply that you’ll check and then message a friend to ask what to do.
Good feedback: Option C is safest: passwords should never be shared. Option D is better than A or B because it delays sharing, but it still misses preserving evidence. Encourage the script: I won’t share my password. I’ll show an adult.

* Award points for steps, not outcomes: give points for saving evidence, reporting correctly, or sending a supportive private message.
* Badges as microcredentials: Make them visible on a classroom board or digital portfolio (with student permission).
* Leaderboards with care: If used, include collaboration and kindness scores and allow optout to avoid shaming.

Families and community partners extend classroom safety and wellbeing beyond school hours. Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Lifelong Digital Citizenship: AgeBased Needs
Overview
Children grow into digital citizens through staged practice and increasing responsibility. This lesson gives teachers and caregivers concrete ways to match guidance to developmental readiness, teach autonomy and decisionmaking, and help young people build a sustainable, responsible online identity.
Developmental progression: risks, responsibilities, and teaching focus
Children (approx. 5–10)
* Typical risks: accidental oversharing (photos, names), contact from strangers, exposure to ageinappropriate content.
* Gradual responsibilities: follow simple rules, ask before sharing, bring adults when worried.
* Adult role: set clear boundaries, provide handson supervision, and model sharing decisions.
* Teaching focus: basic privacy (no personal info), simple password habits, and the “pause and tell” routine.
Tweens (approx. 11–13)
* Typical risks: peer pressure, group chat exclusion, early encounters with scams, first social profiles.
* Gradual responsibilities: practice privacy settings, begin selfmonitoring of behaviour, learn to preserve evidence.
* Adult role: coach decisionmaking, allow monitored autonomy, and roleplay responses to common scenarios.
* Teaching focus: recognising red flags, saving screenshots, consent around photos, and basic reputation thinking.
Teens (approx. 14–17)
* Typical risks: reputational damage, image pressure, more private messaging (ephemeral apps), algorithmic influence.
* Gradual responsibilities: manage public vs private posting, secure accounts independently, mentor younger peers.
* Adult role: shift toward partnership and negotiation, discuss consequences and digital legacy, offer tools and privacy review sessions.
* Teaching focus: media literacy, nuanced consent, advanced account security (MFA, password managers), and reputational foresight.
Young adults (18+)
* Typical risks: realworld consequences (employment, higher education, legal), targeted scams, professional identity management.
* Responsibilities: independent security, informed public presence, critical evaluation of sources, and digital selfadvocacy.
* Adult role: support as consultant and resource; respect autonomy while offering expertise when asked.
* Teaching focus: professional online presence, longterm data management, and helpseeking strategies for serious risks.
Teaching autonomy and decisionmaking — practical strategies
* Graduated release: use a scaffolded plan where responsibilities shift from adultled to independent. For example: adults set rules → guided practice with feedback → negotiated independence with checkins.
* Scenario practice: short, realistic roleplays (1–5 minutes) where students choose and justify an action. Debrief to surface reasoning and alternative options.
* Decision rubrics: teach a simple rubric students can use quickly — e.g., "Who can see this? Could it hurt someone? Is it reversible?" — so choices become routine.
* Safe failures: create controlled exercises where mistakes are lowstakes (mock posts, practice reports) so students learn consequences without real harm.
* Habit scaffolds: combine technical defaults with behavioural cues — for example, make privacy settings a checklist before account creation and require a short reflection before posting publicly.
Digital identity across time
* Short term identity (profiles and nicknames): teach students how handles, avatars and bios communicate values and context. Encourage consistency only where beneficial (e.g., professional portfolios) and deliberate separation when necessary (distinct handles for hobbies vs school work).
* Persistent footprint: stress that images, comments and usernames can be copied and linked later. Use concrete exercises: search for your name and discuss what appears and why.
* Reputation management: practice drafting a short bio suitable for school or future employers, and discuss tradeoffs between visibility and privacy.
* Portfolio thinking: encourage teens and young adults to build purposeful, curated archives (e.g., a public project portfolio) rather than a loose, unguided presence.
Milestone checklists (for educators and families)
Children (5–10) — Starter checklist
* Devices in shared family spaces at night.
* Accounts created with adult supervision; privacy set to the strictest reasonable setting.
* Teach and practise: “Pause, don’t share, tell an adult.”
* One short, guided conversation about what’s okay to post (no school/home details or photos of others without permission).
Tweens (11–13) — Independence checklist
* Review and set privacy controls together on each frequently used app.
* One roleplay session per term: responding to a stranger, a pressure request, or a suspicious link.
* Enable MFA on email and primary accounts; introduce a password manager or shared family vault.
* Agree a weekly checkin: students show one app and explain recent interactions.
Teens (14–17) — Responsibility checklist
* Teach and practise account hygiene: unique passwords, 2FA, recovery plans.
* Host a portfolio review: draft a short professional bio and remove or archive three posts that could harm future opportunities.
* Run an experiment: twoweek reduction of a chosen app or hiding like counts; reflect on mood and behaviour.
* Train as peer supporters: have students practise bystander scripts and incident reporting with credits or badges.
Young adults (18+) — Transition checklist
* Audit major accounts for professional suitability and privacy settings.
* Move critical recovery info to a secure manager and set up hardwarebacked MFA where possible.
* Create a personal data plan: what to keep, archive or delete, and how to manage public portfolios or LinkedIn profiles.
* Know where to seek help: platform appeal processes, legal advice for clear doxxing/defamation, and mentalhealth resources.
Conversation prompts by stage
* Children: "Show me one thing you liked online today — who else sees it?"
* Tweens: "If someone you don’t know asks for a photo, what would you do first?"
* Teens: "Would you be comfortable if a teacher or future employer saw this post? Why or why not?"
* Young adults: "Which pieces of your online presence should stay public, and which should be private?"
Assessment and reflection tools
* Quick exit ticket: a onesentence decision a student would make in a presented scenario and why. Use this to target followup coaching.
* Twoweek experiments: collect student reflections (short journal entries) and a oneline metric (mood scale) to evaluate wellbeing impacts.
* Portfolio review rubric: clarity, professionalism, privacy risk, and evidence of learning — useful for older teens preparing for work or college.
Implementation tips for educators and caregivers
* Make transitions explicit: announce when a student’s responsibilities are shifting and cocreate the conditions (e.g., trial duration, checkin schedule).
* Keep interventions short and practical: threeminute privacy checks, fiveminute roleplays, and onepage checklists are more likely to stick than long lectures.
* Build visible rituals: monthly settings checks, a termly portfolio review, and a shared class protocol for reporting incidents.
Teach the metaskill: good digital decisions
Rather than only teaching platform rules, teach a fast decision routine students can apply anywhere: "Who can see this? Could this be used against me or someone else? Is it reversible? If unsure, pause and ask a trusted adult." This metaskill transfers across apps and life stages.
Closing
Helping young people grow into thoughtful digital citizens means guiding them from dependent rulefollowing to independent, reflective decisionmaking. Use staged milestones, scaffolded practice, and frequent, short reflections so responsibility grows with capability — and so digital identity becomes a deliberate, manageable part of growing up.

Purpose and outcomes
A capstone project turns safety and citizenship learning into purposeful action. The aim is for students to demonstrate practical skills (privacy, reporting, bystander support, technical settings), collaborate with peers, and produce a tangible product or event that benefits their school or community.

Digital Citizenship Week (event)

Peer Mentor Program (ongoing)

* What it is: students audit existing school policies and propose evidencebased improvements (monitoring, reporting, restorative practices).

* Primary (5–10): keep projects short and concrete. Example: create a poster series and run a short assembly with teacher support.
* Middle (11–13): small group projects with teacher checkins. Example: design a oneweek campaign and lead one family clinic.
* Upper secondary (14–18): multiweek projects with public deliverables and evaluation. Expect independent research, stakeholder outreach and presentations.
Project planning template (copy and adapt)
* Title and summary: one sentence describing what you will do and why.
* Learning goals: 2–3 skills or competencies students will demonstrate.
* Audience & impact: who benefits and how will you measure that benefit?
* Stakeholders: list staff, families, community partners and student roles.

Scoring guidance

* Keep it audienceappropriate: 5–8 minutes for classroom; 8–12 minutes for a school assembly.

* Rehearse with Q&A: practise likely questions and a twoline response for tricky issues (privacy, escalation).

* Privacy first: if projects involve student data or images, get explicit consent and anonymise results where possible.

* Midpoint check: review evidence and provide formative feedback using the rubric.
* Final week: rehearse presentations, confirm permissions, prepare handoff documentation.
* Postproject: collect evaluations, summarise impact, and schedule a short retrospective with students.
Start with clear, small wins
Choose a project scope that guarantees a visible outcome (one workshop, one toolkit page, a poster campaign). Small, wellexecuted projects build confidence and are easier to sustain or scale next year.

Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Evaluation, Data & Continuous Improvement
Why evaluate?
Measuring what you teach and how students, families and staff respond turns good intentions into reliable practice. Evaluation helps you know what’s working, where to focus limited time and resources, and how to improve lessons, policies and supports over time.
Core evaluation questions to start with
* What change do we expect (knowledge, behaviour, culture) and by when?
* How will we recognise that change (indicators, numbers, stories)?
* Who will collect the data, how often, and where will it be stored?
Simple tools you can use right away
* Short surveys
* Who: students, families, staff.
* When: pre/post lessons, after workshops, termly checkins.
* Keep them brief (5–8 items) and mix question types: multiple choice for quick trend measurement and one short freetext for nuance.
Sample student survey (5 items)
* I know how to save a screenshot and preserve evidence if I see something worrying. (Strongly disagree → Strongly agree)
* I feel confident reporting harassment or a safety concern to an adult. (Likert)
* In the past month, I changed a privacy setting on an app. (Yes / No)
* Which action would you take first if you received a scary message? (Choices: reply / block / save screenshot & tell adult / delete)
* One thing that made me feel safer online this term is: __________ (short answer)
Family and staff surveys should mirror student themes but focus on observable behaviours (e.g., number of settings checks completed, requests for help) and satisfaction with school responses.
* Behaviour indicators (what to count)
* Process metrics (easy wins): number of privacy checks completed, MFA enabled on school accounts, number of families attending workshops.
* Outcome metrics (behaviour change): % of students who report they would save evidence, reduction in repeated incidents of the same type, increase in bystander support actions logged.
* Safety metrics: number of incidents reported, number of incidents escalated to safeguarding, time from report to initial adult contact.
* Incident tracking
* Use a simple template or spreadsheet with these columns: Date, Platform, Student(s) involved (anonymised ID), Incident type (harassment / doxxing / NSFW / grooming), Evidence saved (Y/N), Actions taken (blocked / reported / referred to safeguarding), Outcome (resolved / ongoing), Followup date, Notes.
* Track both counts and narratives: numbers show trends; short anonymised case notes preserve context necessary for fair decisions.
From data to improvement — a practical cycle
Use a fast PlanDoStudyAct (PDSA) loop:
* Plan: pick one focused question (e.g., “Does a 15minute roleplay increase students’ confidence in preserving evidence?”) and define the metric.
* Do: run the activity with a class or pilot group and collect pre/post data.
* Study: compare results, look for unexpected patterns (false positives, equity gaps) and read freetext for nuance.
* Act: refine the lesson, change the script, or expand the activity to more classes. Repeat the loop.
Analysis basics (keep it simple)
* Use counts and percentages for trends (e.g., % students enabling MFA).
* Compare pre/post survey means or % change for key items.
* Segment by group where relevant (year group, program participation) to spot inequities.
* Read qualitative responses for context and illustrative quotes to explain the numbers.
Ethics and data privacy (nonnegotiable)
* Always minimise: collect only what you need.
* Anonymise or pseudonymise student identifiers before sharing broadly.
* Store sensitive incident evidence securely and limit access to trained staff.
* Get consent where required and be transparent with families about what data you collect and why.
Protect privacy and dignity
Do not publish or circulate identifiable screenshots, names or sensitive incident details. Share anonymised summaries and limit raw evidence access to designated safeguarding staff and platform support when needed.
Using data to refine lessons and policies
* Look for gaps between confidence and behaviour. For example, if many students say they know how to save evidence but few actually do it when incidents occur, the fix is practice drills, not another lecture.
* Prioritise changes that address the largest or riskiest gaps first (e.g., if reporting rates are low for serious incidents, simplify the reporting route and rehearse it in class).
* Use A/B tests for small changes: try two different scripts or templates and see which leads to faster reporting or better preserved evidence.
* Track unintended consequences: a tightening of monitoring may reduce incidents but increase student anxiety — use surveys to measure both safety and wellbeing.
Reporting impact to stakeholders
Who needs what
* Leadership & governors: highlevel trends, policy implications, resource asks.
* Teachers & staff: actionable classroom insights and short checklists they can implement.
* Families: clear takeaways and simple actions they can do at home.
* Students: ageappropriate summaries and invitations to participate in next steps.
Formats that work
* Onepage brief: headline metric + 3 short bullets (What changed, Why it matters, Next steps).
* Dashboards: a small set of KPIs (trend line for reported incidents, % completed safety checks, average response time) with contextual notes. Keep dashboards simple and update monthly or termly.
* Case studies: 1–2 anonymised stories that illustrate how a change helped a student and what was learned.
* Slides for meetings: 3–5 slides — summary, one chart, one learning, proposed action.
Suggested KPIs
* Number of safety incidents reported (trend).
* Median time from report to first adult contact.
* % students completing a settings check in the last term.
* % of incidents with complete preserved evidence.
* Student selfreported confidence in one key skill (pre/post).
How to tell the story
* Start with the headline: “Reports of harassment fell 15% this term after adding roleplay drills.”
* Explain the data simply: show the relevant chart, the sample size, and one sentence on how you measured it.
* Offer next steps and resource needs: “We recommend expanding the drill to Year 9; this requires a halfday teacher training.”
Quick starter plan (30/60/90 days)
* 30 days: pick 3 simple metrics (incidents reported, settings checks completed, student confidence on one skill). Run a short prelesson survey and start incident tracking in a shared spreadsheet.
* 60 days: run one PDSA cycle on a targeted activity (e.g., evidencecapture drill). Analyse pre/post results and collect teacher feedback.
* 90 days: prepare a onepage report for leadership with trends, one case study, and recommended next steps. Share an anonymised dashboard with staff and families.
Start small and iterate
Choose a tiny set of measurable questions and build habit around collecting them. Small, regular data beats a single large study — it gives you a steady rhythm of improvement and makes change manageable.
Closing
Evaluation doesn’t need to be complex to be useful. Use short surveys, clear behaviour indicators and a simple incident log; run fast improvement cycles; protect privacy; and report concise, actionable findings to stakeholders. Over time, these small routines create clearer, fairer and more effective safety practice across school and home.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Implementation Roadmap & Sustainability
Why this matters
An implementation roadmap turns good ideas into durable practice. A clear rollout, realistic training, modest resourcing and builtin renewal cycles help schools and community groups maintain safety, privacy and wellbeing interventions over time — without overwhelming staff or families.
Phased rollout: a practical, lowrisk approach
Phase 0 — Prepare (2–4 weeks)
* Form a small steering group (lead teacher, counsellor/safeguarding lead, IT contact, parent rep, student rep).
* Audit existing efforts: policies, monitoring tools, past incident logs and any training already delivered.
* Define success: pick 2–4 measurable outcomes for the first year (e.g., % students who enable MFA, # of preserved evidence incidents, % staff trained).
* Create a onepage communications plan explaining purpose, scope and key dates.
Phase 1 — Pilot (6–8 weeks)
* Choose a limited scope: one year group or one extracurricular cohort.
* Deliver core activities: a short lesson on privacy & reporting, a 30minute roleplay drill, and a family settings clinic.
* Gather feedback: quick surveys for students, staff and families; track incidents and evidence quality.
* Refine materials and training based on pilot results.
Phase 2 — Scale (8–12 weeks)
* Roll out to all year groups with staggered starts to manage staff load.
* Provide teacher toolkits (slides, scripts, quick activities) and short training sessions.
* Run parallel family workshops and publish a onepage checklist online.
* Monitor KPIs weekly and hold short weekly checkins for the steering group.
Phase 3 — Embed & sustain (ongoing)
* Add a recurring calendar of refreshers, student leadership activities and annual reviews.
* Integrate short routines into school rhythms (registration, assemblies, tutor time).
* Publish a short annual report with metrics and next steps.
Sample 90day rollout checklist (copy & adapt)
* Week 1: Form steering group; set success metrics.
* Weeks 2–3: Pilot materials developed; select pilot class.
* Week 4: Train pilot teachers (60–90 minutes).
* Weeks 5–8: Run pilot activities and family clinic; collect feedback.
* Week 9: Analyse pilot data; update materials.
* Weeks 10–12: Staggered broader rollout begins; schedule refresher calendar.
Training schedules: who needs what and when
Teacher & staff training (recommended cadence)
* Initial staff briefing (60 minutes): purpose, policy summary, how to respond to reports, preservation basics.
* Practical workshop (90 minutes): roleplays, report drafting, use of any monitoring dashboards, and escalation flow.
* Annual refresher (45 minutes): new incidents, updated tools, case study debrief.
Student training (ageadaptable)
* Core lesson (30–45 minutes): an interactive lesson on privacy, triage (Pause—Save—Tell) and bystander scripts.
* Skills lab (30 minutes): roleplays or a short escaperoom style drill to practise evidence preservation and reporting.
* Peer leader training (2 × 60 minutes): for students who will mentor others or run settings clinics.
Family & community sessions
* Short settings clinic (30–45 minutes): live walkthrough of one popular app and a printable checklist families can take home.
* Evening workshop (60–90 minutes): overview of risks, two practical actions to try that week, and signups for onetoone tech help.
* Quarterly dropin Q&A (30 minutes): optional, lowpressure support for families.
Sample teacher workshop outline (90 minutes)
* Opening & goals (10 min)
* Short primer on common incidents and evidence (15 min)
* Roleplay rotations (30 min) — bystander scripts / preserving evidence / reporting
* Tool demo and escalation flow (20 min)
* Q&A and immediate next steps (15 min)
Resource lists and budget considerations
Core resource categories
* People time: planning meetings, training time for staff and student leaders, and facilitator hours for family workshops.
* Materials: printed handouts, poster printing, badges/certificates for student leaders.
* Technical: any classroom management or incident tracking tools, basic subscriptions, or secure storage for evidence.
* External support: facilitator fees for specialist workshops or translation services for family sessions.
Sample modest budget (annual, illustrative)
Item | Typical cost range
Printing & handouts | $100–$500
Teacher training cover (supply teacher) | $150–$300 per session
Incident tracking tool / simple SaaS | $0–$1,500
External facilitator (family workshop) | $200–$800 per event
Student leader badges / materials | $50–$200
Budget tips
* Start with lowcost or free tools (school Google Drive, printed onepagers) and pilot before buying subscriptions.
* Leverage community partners to run workshops in exchange for visibility or small grants.
* Budget for staff cover during training — this is often the largest single cost and easy to overlook.
Sourcing and procurement
* Use existing vendor relationships for technical tools and ask for education discounts.
* Pilot new tech with a small group before wholeschool procurement.
* Document datahandling expectations in contracts (retention, access, deletion) and seek tools that respect student privacy.
Maintaining momentum: refreshers, student leadership and reviews
Regular refreshers
* Microrefreshers (5–10 minutes): short activities in tutor time once a fortnight (scenario of the week, 1question polls).
* Termly drills (20–30 minutes): evidence preservation, roleplay of reporting flows, or a quick settings check.
* Annual deep review (60 minutes): update staff on shifts in platform risks, new policies and any changes to tools.
Student leadership & peer support
* Roles and responsibilities: peer mentors help run settings clinics, lead small roleplay sessions, and act as a confidential first contact in nonemergency cases.
* Selection and training: optin application, twosession training (peer support basics, safeguarding boundaries), and supervised practice.
* Recognition: badges, certificates, and small privileges (organise a wellbeing event) to sustain motivation.
Sample student leader role description (short)
* Title: Digital Peer Mentor
* Time commitment: ~1 hour/week + monthly meeting
* Duties: run one settings clinic per term, lead one 20minute roleplay session, promote the school’s reporting routine.
* Boundaries: not a counsellor — escalate safeguarding concerns to adults immediately.
Annual review and continuous improvement
* Data review: compare KPIs yearonyear (incident counts, time to first contact, % trained).
* Stakeholder feedback: short surveys from students, staff and families and one focused feedback session with student leaders.
* Policy refresh: update the implementation plan, training materials and any procurement decisions based on evidence.
* Publish an annual onepage summary for the community with outcomes and next steps.
Sustainability practices
* Bake safety into existing rhythms: attach a 5minute settings check to enrolment week, include a oneline digital wellbeing check in tutor logs, and add a safety metric to school improvement plans.
* Rotate leadership: put student leader roles on a termly rotation to widen participation and reduce burnout.
* Maintain a living resource folder (Shared Drive > Digital Safety) with current slide decks, scripts and the latest checklists.
Measuring success (practical indicators)
* Process: % staff trained, % classes that ran the core lesson, number of family workshop attendees.
* Behaviour: % students who completed a privacy/settings check each term, % of incidents with complete preserved evidence.
* Impact: reductions in repeat incidents of the same type, faster response times, and qualitative reports of improved confidence from students and staff.
Quick start tips
* Start small: pilot with one cohort and one toolset before committing large budgets.
* Prioritise people: invest in short, practical training for the adults who will respond to incidents.
* Make it routine: short, repeated activities win over oneoff events.
Iterate quickly and visibly
Run a short pilot, collect simple data, share what you learned, and adapt. Visible iteration builds trust and makes larger rollouts easier.
Watch for staff overload
New programmes often depend on a few committed staff. Protect them by scheduling time, limiting additional duties, and rotating responsibilities so momentum is sustainable.
Final note
An effective implementation plan balances clarity with flexibility: clear phases and measurable goals make progress visible, while regular refreshers, student involvement and a modest, welltargeted budget keep the work sustainable over time.
Cybersecurity for Kids v1 - Reflection Prompts & Next Steps for Educators
Why reflection matters
Teaching and safeguarding work improves when it’s intentional: short, regular reflection turns isolated events into lasting improvements. Use these prompts and templates to turn observations into specific actions, communicate clearly with families and leaders, and plan focused professional development.
Guided reflection questions (use in staff meetings or individually)
* What went well? Name one concrete thing that helped students feel safer or more able to act.
* What surprised you? Describe one outcome you didn’t expect (positive or negative).
* What evidence did you collect? List artifacts (screenshots, incident logs, student reflections, attendance at workshops).
* Where did we fall short? Identify one gap in guidance, followthrough, or resources.
* Who benefited most, and who might have been left out? Consider equity and access.
* What immediate change would make the biggest difference next term?
A short facilitation plan for reflection (20 minutes)
* Silent review (5 min): Everyone reads a short incident summary or a recent class activity and makes two quick notes: one success, one learning.
* Pair share (7 min): In pairs, share notes and pick one idea each pair thinks the whole staff should hear.
* Wholegroup prioritise (8 min): Each pair names their idea; the group votes (thumbs up) on up to three actions to trial next term.

Pick one reflection question ("What went well?") and one concrete small action to try next term. Small cycles of practice and review build momentum far faster than big oneoff plans.
Closing — using reflection to drive continuous improvement
Regular reflection, focused action planning and clear communication close the loop between classroom practice and schoollevel policy.
